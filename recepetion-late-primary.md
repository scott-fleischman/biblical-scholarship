Below is a **primary-source map from the late-medieval / Reformation threshold to the present**, focused on **textual reception history**: translations, printed Bibles, confessions, catechisms, liturgies, polemics, official ecclesial statements, Jewish communal statements, modern study Bibles, digital textual platforms, and major interpretive works that became reception artifacts.

I am treating “primary source” broadly: a modern scholar’s book is usually secondary scholarship, but **Wellhausen, Spinoza, Bultmann, Barth, Heschel, Kaplan, Cone, Schüssler Fiorenza, etc. become primary sources if the research question is “how did modern Jews and Christians receive the Bible?”**

**Key**

**[E] Essential** — first-order reception artifact.
**[I] Important** — significant, often tradition-specific.
**[H] Historically decisive** — changed later reception, even if now contested.
**[T] Textual witness / edition / translation** — Bible text, critical edition, printed edition, digital corpus.
**[C] Confessional / conciliar / doctrinal** — creed, confession, catechism, council, official statement.
**[L] Liturgical** — prayer book, lectionary, hymnbook, rite, siddur, mahzor.
**[P] Polemical / interreligious** — Jewish-Christian, Christian-Muslim, intra-Christian, anti-critical, anti-modernist, apologetic.
**[V] Vernacular / popular / artistic** — literary, visual, musical, dramatic, digital, or lay reception.
**[M] Modern interpretive landmark** — modern work that functions as primary evidence for reception history.

# 1. Late-medieval / early-print threshold, c. 1450–1520

These are hinge texts: still medieval in many ways, but they create the material world in which Reformation, Catholic renewal, Jewish print culture, and modern textual criticism emerge.

## Printed and manuscript Bible thresholds

* **[T/H] Gutenberg Bible, c. 1450s** — printed Latin Vulgate; technological watershed for Western Christian Bible reception.
* **[T/H] Soncino Hebrew Bible, 1488** — first complete printed Hebrew Bible.
* **[T/E] Bomberg Rabbinic Bible / Mikraot Gedolot, 1516–17; second Bomberg Rabbinic Bible, 1524–25** — printed Hebrew Bible with masorah, targum, and medieval Jewish commentaries; foundational for later Jewish and Christian Hebraist study.
* **[T/H] Complutensian Polyglot, printed 1514–17, published 1520** — Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and Aramaic textual display; one of the decisive early modern textual artifacts.
* **[T/H] Erasmus, *Novum Instrumentum omne*, 1516** — Greek New Testament with new Latin translation and annotations; decisive for Reformation-era textual reception.
* **[T/H] Aldine Septuagint, 1518** — first printed complete Greek Old Testament / Septuagint.
* **[T/H] Valla, *Annotationes in Novum Testamentum*** — fifteenth-century philological critique of the Latin NT against Greek evidence; printed in the early sixteenth century and important for humanist biblical criticism.
* **[T/H] Reuchlin, *De rudimentis hebraicis*, 1506** — Christian Hebrew grammar/lexicon; major Christian Hebraist artifact.
* **[T/I] Paris, Venice, Basel, Antwerp, and Plantin polyglot traditions** — continue the polyglot reception of Scripture.

## Late medieval sources that continue into early modernity

* **[E/H] Nicholas of Lyra, *Postilla litteralis super totam Bibliam*** — late medieval literal commentary deeply influential into the Reformation.
* **[T/E] Glossa Ordinaria** — medieval Latin Bible surrounded by patristic and medieval glosses; remains a reference point in early print.
* **[I] Paul of Burgos, *Additiones* to Nicholas of Lyra** — converso Christian supplementation of Lyra.
* **[I] Matthias Döring, *Replicae*** — defense of Lyra against Paul of Burgos.
* **[V/I] Wycliffite Bible tradition** — pre-Reformation English vernacular Bible reception.
* **[V/I] Czech Bible traditions before and around the Hussite movement** — important for vernacular reformist reception.
* **[L/I] Late medieval missals, breviaries, books of hours, passion meditations** — Scripture received through liturgical and devotional cycles.

# 2. Reformation-era Bible translations and paratexts

These are among the most important primary sources for modern biblical reception because **translation choices, prefaces, marginal notes, book order, canon boundaries, and chapter headings became confessional acts**.

* **[T/E/H] Luther, *September Testament*, 1522** — German NT with programmatic prefaces and translation choices.
* **[T/E/H] Luther Bible, 1534 and later revisions** — decisive German Protestant Bible.
* **[M/E] Luther’s biblical prefaces** — especially Romans, James, Revelation, Psalms; crucial for canon and theological reception.
* **[V/I] Low German Luther Bible traditions** — regional Protestant reception.
* **[T/I] Zurich Bible, 1531** — Zwinglian/Reformed German Bible.

## Core confessional and doctrinal sources

* **[C/E] Melanchthon, *Apology of the Augsburg Confession***.
* **[C/E] Luther, *Small Catechism* and *Large Catechism***.

* **[C/E] *Treatise on the Power and Primacy of the Pope***.
* **[C/E] *Formula of Concord*, 1577**.
* **[C/E] *Book of Concord*, 1580** — collection of Lutheran confessional writings, including the ecumenical creeds, Augsburg Confession, Apology, catechisms, Smalcald Articles, and Formula of Concord. ([Book of Concord][2])

* **[M/E] Luther, *Lectures on Romans*, *Galatians Commentary*, *Lectures on Genesis*, *Bondage of the Will***.

* **[L/E] Luther’s hymns: “A Mighty Fortress,” catechetical hymns, psalm paraphrases**.
* **[M/I] Melanchthon, *Loci communes*** — early Protestant dogmatic reception of Scripture.
* **[P/I] Flacius Illyricus, *Clavis Scripturae Sacrae*** — Protestant hermeneutical tool.
* **[M/I] Chemnitz, *Examination of the Council of Trent*** — Lutheran reception of Catholic conciliar biblical claims.

* **[L/I] Lutheran church orders and agendas** — Scripture through worship and catechesis.
* **[L/I] Bach cantatas and passions** — Lutheran biblical-liturgical-musical reception.

# 5. Anglican primary sources

Anglican reception is unusually textual-liturgical: Scripture is received through **public reading, lectionary, collects, homilies, articles, and prayer-book order**.

* **[L/C/E/H] *Book of Common Prayer*, 1549, 1552, 1559, 1662** — core Anglican liturgical reception.
* **[C/E] Thirty-Nine Articles, finalized 1563/1571** — the Church of England presents the Articles as agreed by bishops and clergy in Convocation in 1562; Article VI is especially important for canon and sufficiency of Scripture. ([Church of England][4])
* **[C/I] *Book of Homilies*** — officially authorized scriptural preaching.
* **[T/E] King James Version, 1611** — Anglican and English-speaking Protestant textual reception.
* **[M/I] Cranmer, prefaces, homilies, liturgical compositions**.
* **[P/I] John Jewel, *Apology of the Church of England***.
* **[M/E] Richard Hooker, *Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity*** — Scripture, reason, tradition, church polity.
* **[M/I] Lancelot Andrewes, sermons** — patristic and biblical Anglican preaching.
* **[M/I] Jeremy Taylor, devotional and moral works**.
* **[C/I] Lambeth Articles, 1595** — Calvinist reception within Anglican controversy.
* **[C/E] Chicago-Lambeth Quadrilateral, 1886/1888** — Scripture, creeds, sacraments, episcopate as ecumenical Anglican identity.
* **[L/I] Episcopal *Book of Common Prayer*, 1789, 1928, 1979**.
* **[L/I] Church of England, *Common Worship***.
* **[C/I] Lambeth Conference resolutions** — modern Anglican reception of Scripture in ecclesial debate.

# 7. Roman Catholic primary sources, Trent to present

## Trent, Vulgate, and post-Tridentine reception

* **[C/E/H] Council of Trent, Session IV, 1546: Decree on Canonical Scriptures and Decree on Edition and Use of Sacred Books** — decisive Catholic response to Reformation disputes over canon, tradition, and the Vulgate. Trent receives the books of both Testaments and apostolic traditions “with equal affection of piety and reverence.” ([Papal Encyclicals][5])
* **[C/E] Roman Catechism / Catechism of the Council of Trent, 1566**.
* **[T/E] Sixtine Vulgate, 1590; Sixto-Clementine Vulgate, 1592** — official Latin Catholic Bible tradition after Trent.
* **[L/E] Roman Missal of Pius V, 1570**.
* **[L/E] Roman Breviary and Roman Ritual**.
* **[T/E] Douay-Rheims Bible** — English Catholic reception.
* **[M/I] Robert Bellarmine, *Disputationes de controversiis*** — Catholic polemical reception of Scripture and tradition.
* **[M/I] Cornelius a Lapide, biblical commentaries**.
* **[M/I] Maldonado, Gospel commentaries**.
* **[M/I] Calmet, Bible dictionary and commentary**.

## Catholic modern magisterial reception of Scripture

* **[C/E] Vatican I, *Dei Filius*, 1870** — revelation, faith, reason, Scripture.
* **[C/E] Leo XIII, *Providentissimus Deus*, 1893** — Catholic response to historical criticism.
* **[C/E] Pius X, *Lamentabili sane* and *Pascendi dominici gregis*, 1907** — anti-modernist reception of biblical criticism.
* **[C/E] Pius XII, *Divino afflante Spiritu*, 1943** — major opening toward Catholic historical-critical biblical scholarship.
* **[C/E/H] Vatican II, *Dei Verbum*, 1965** — central modern Catholic document on revelation, Scripture, tradition, inspiration, interpretation, and ecclesial reading. ([Vatican][6])
* **[C/E] Vatican II, *Sacrosanctum Concilium*** — lectionary and liturgical reception.
* **[C/E/P] Vatican II, *Nostra Aetate*, 1965** — decisive for Catholic reception of Judaism and shared scriptural patrimony. ([Vatican][7])
* **[C/E] Pontifical Biblical Commission, *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church*, 1993** — official Catholic reception of modern interpretive methods; the Vatican document index lists it as a 1993 PBC document. ([Vatican][8])
* **[C/E/P] Pontifical Biblical Commission, *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*, 2001/2002** — major Catholic source for Jewish-Christian scriptural reception. ([Vatican][9])
* **[C/E] Benedict XVI, *Verbum Domini*, 2010** — post-synodal apostolic exhortation on the Word of God.
* **[C/E] Francis, *Aperuit illis*, 2019** — establishes Sunday of the Word of God.
* **[C/E] *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, 1992/1997** — modern Catholic synthesis of scriptural doctrine.

## Catholic Bible translations and liturgical texts

* **[T/E] Jerusalem Bible, 1966; New Jerusalem Bible, 1985; Revised New Jerusalem Bible, 2019**.
* **[T/E] New American Bible, 1970; NABRE, 2011** — USCCB states that the NABRE was released in 2011 after nearly twenty years of work by scholars, bishops, revisers, and editors. ([USCCB][10])
* **[T/I] Knox Bible, 1945–49**.

* **[L/I] Liturgy of the Hours / *Liturgia Horarum***.
* **[L/I] Revised Grail Psalms / Abbey Psalms and Canticles**.
