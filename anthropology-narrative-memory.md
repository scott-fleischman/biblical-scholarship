Below is an **additive pass** on **anthropology, narrative, and social memory**. I am avoiding references already foregrounded earlier, including Alter, Sternberg, Berlin, Bar-Efrat, Fokkelman, Rhoads/Dewey/Michie, Culpepper, Tannehill, Dunn, Rodríguez, Kelber, Douglas, Bell, Geertz, Turner, Asad, Bourdieu, Foucault, Orsi, McGuire, Bielo, Luhrmann, and other items already named.

**Key**

**[E] Essential** — should be known by a serious researcher.
**[I] Important** — specialized, influential, or useful for a subfield.
**[H] Historical classic** — older, sometimes superseded, but field-shaping.
**[M] Method / theory** — general conceptual framework.
**[BIB] Biblical / Jewish / Christian application.**
**[ANE] Ancient Near Eastern / Greco-Roman application.**
**[NE] Non-English or original-language control.**

# 1. Anthropology: general theory and method

These works help prevent a narrow “text-only” approach. They ask what kinship, ritual, gift, debt, shame, honor, violence, purity, household, memory, embodiment, and social identity do in actual communities.

* **[M/E] Alan Barnard and Jonathan Spencer, eds., *The Routledge Encyclopedia of Social and Cultural Anthropology*** — quick control for anthropological concepts, theorists, schools, and debates. Useful when a biblical scholar invokes “liminality,” “gift,” “kinship,” “ritual,” “honor,” or “symbol” without full disciplinary context. Routledge describes the encyclopedia as combining anthropological theory and ethnography, with substantial entries, biographies, and glossary items. ([Routledge][1])

* **[M/E] James Carrier and Deborah Gewertz, eds., *The Handbook of Sociocultural Anthropology*** — broad current orientation to the discipline, especially after 1980: changing theory, institutions, regions, and methods. It is useful for moving beyond older “Mediterranean honor-shame” models into contemporary anthropology. ([Routledge][2])

* **[M/H] Marcel Mauss, *The Gift*** — essential for gift exchange, obligation, reciprocity, patronage, sacrifice, grace, debt, and covenantal sociality. For biblical studies, it is a background text for discussions of gift/grace, patron-client relations, almsgiving, temple offerings, and benefaction.

* **[M/E] Maurice Godelier, *The Enigma of the Gift*** — a major update and critique of Mauss. Adds attention to what cannot be given away: sacred objects, inalienable possessions, ancestral property, and symbolic power.

* **[M/H] Arnold van Gennep, *The Rites of Passage*** — foundational for initiation, transition, purity, pilgrimage, conversion, baptism, ordination, mourning, and festival processions.

* **[M/H] Claude Lévi-Strauss, *Structural Anthropology*; *The Savage Mind*** — still important for myth, binary opposition, classification, kinship, and structural analysis. Use carefully; historically decisive but not a sufficient contemporary method.

* **[M/E] Marshall Sahlins, *Culture and Practical Reason*; *Stone Age Economics*; *Historical Metaphors and Mythical Realities*** — important for economy, myth, practice, and historical transformation. Useful for avoiding crude materialist or purely ideological readings of biblical social life.

* **[M/E] Roy Rappaport, *Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity*** — one of the most important anthropological theories of ritual, sanctity, and social order. Useful for sacrifice, covenant ceremonies, liturgy, oath, and public ritual.

* **[M/E] Stanley Tambiah, *Magic, Science, Religion, and the Scope of Rationality*; *Culture, Thought, and Social Action*** — crucial for ritual speech, magic, performativity, and symbolic action.

* **[M/I] Humphrey and Laidlaw, *The Archetypal Actions of Ritual*** — strong theory of ritualization and intentionality; useful for priestly ritual, monastic gestures, liturgical performance, and rabbinic practice.

* **[M/I] Ronald Grimes, *Ritual Criticism*** — practical method for analyzing ritual events, not only ritual texts.

* **[M/E] Harvey Whitehouse, *Arguments and Icons*; *Modes of Religiosity*** — influential cognitive-anthropological account of high-arousal imagistic religion vs routinized doctrinal religion. Useful for comparing Passover, baptism, martyrdom, pilgrimage, monastic discipline, revival, and daily liturgy.

* **[M/I] Pascal Boyer, *Religion Explained*** — cognitive-anthropological model of religion. Useful as a methodological foil, especially for divine agency, spirits, demons, ancestors, and ritual.

* **[M/I] Stewart Guthrie, *Faces in the Clouds*** — classic cognitive account of anthropomorphism and agency detection.

* **[M/I] Justin Barrett, *Why Would Anyone Believe in God?*** — cognitive science of religion; useful for contemporary theory, less directly for ancient texts.

# 2. Anthropology of Mediterranean and ancient social worlds

Earlier lists covered some social-scientific biblical works, but this more concentrated shelf adds the anthropological roots and the better controls.

## Honor, shame, agonism, and public reputation

* **[M/H] J. G. Peristiany, ed., *Honour and Shame: The Values of Mediterranean Society*** — historical classic behind much biblical “honor-shame” interpretation. Use with critique; it shaped the field but can overgeneralize.

* **[M/H] Julian Pitt-Rivers, *The People of the Sierra*** — classic ethnography of honor, village life, patronage, and masculinity in Mediterranean anthropology.

* **[M/I] David Gilmore, ed., *Honor and Shame and the Unity of the Mediterranean*** — important because it both extends and problematizes the “Mediterranean honor-shame” model.

* **[M/I] Michael Herzfeld, *The Poetics of Manhood*; *Anthropology through the Looking-Glass*** — more reflexive and critical approach to Mediterranean honor, masculinity, nationalism, and ethnographic construction.

* **[BIB/I] John J. Pilch, *The Cultural World of Jesus* series** — short, social-scientific essays on healing, kinship, purity, honor, family, and village life in Gospel contexts.

* **[BIB/I] Richard L. Rohrbaugh, ed., *The Social Sciences and New Testament Interpretation*** — useful collection mapping social-scientific approaches before the more recent identity/memory turn.

## Kinship, household, lineage, and family

* **[M/E] Fredrik Barth, ed., *Ethnic Groups and Boundaries*** — foundational for ethnicity as boundary-making rather than biological essence. Essential for Israel/Judah, Jews/Judeans, Samaritans, Greeks, Romans, Christians, and sectarian groups.

* **[M/E] David Schneider, *American Kinship*; *A Critique of the Study of Kinship*** — important for questioning inherited assumptions about “family” and “blood.”

* **[M/I] Marshall Sahlins, *What Kinship Is—And Is Not*** — useful for kinship as mutuality of being; relevant to covenant, adoption, fictive kinship, church family, and monastic brotherhood.

* **[BIB/E] J. David Schloen, *The House of the Father as Fact and Symbol*** — major social-archaeological and anthropological model for patrimonial household structures in Ugarit and ancient Israel.

* **[BIB/I] Lawrence Stager, “The Archaeology of the Family in Ancient Israel”** — article-level classic on the four-room house, family structure, and Israelite social organization.

* **[BIB/I] Philip King and Lawrence Stager, *Life in Biblical Israel*** — material-social reconstruction of household, economy, food, family, and village life.

* **[BIB/I] Victor Matthews and Don Benjamin, *Social World of Ancient Israel, 1250–587 BCE*** — useful for students: kinship, economy, gender, law, war, and everyday life.

## Gift, patronage, debt, redistribution

* **[M/E] Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation*; *Trade and Market in the Early Empires*** — crucial for embedded economies, redistribution, reciprocity, and ancient market caution.

* **[M/I] David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*** — sweeping and debated, but useful for thinking about debt, obligation, slavery, forgiveness, and moral economy.

* **[M/I] Chris Gregory, *Gifts and Commodities*** — strong anthropological contrast between gift and commodity economies.

* **[BIB/I] Zeba Crook, *Reconcepualising Conversion: Patronage, Loyalty, and Conversion in the Religions of the Ancient Mediterranean*** — reframes conversion in patronage and loyalty terms.

* **[BIB/I] John K. Chow, *Patronage and Power*** — important for patronage and 1 Corinthians.

* **[BIB/I] Alicia Batten, *Friendship and Benefaction in James*** — friendship, benefaction, moral exhortation, and social relations.

## Peasants, domination, resistance, hidden transcripts

* **[M/E] James C. Scott, *Weapons of the Weak*** — everyday resistance, hidden action, and peasant politics.

* **[M/E] James C. Scott, *Domination and the Arts of Resistance*** — “hidden transcripts” and public/private discourse; useful for apocalyptic, parables, coded resistance, and satire.

* **[M/E] James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State*** — state simplification, legibility, taxation, cadastral control, and imperial administration.

* **[BIB/I] K. C. Hanson and Douglas Oakman, *Palestine in the Time of Jesus*** — anthropological-economic reconstruction of Galilean/Judean social life.

* **[BIB/I] Douglas Oakman, *Jesus and the Peasants*** — peasant studies and historical Jesus context.

* **[BIB/I] William Herzog, *Parables as Subversive Speech*** — parables read through exploitation, debt, and peasant resistance.

# 3. Social identity, groupness, ethnicity, and boundaries

This is partly anthropology, partly social psychology, and now a major biblical-studies method.

## General theory

* **[M/E] Henri Tajfel, *Human Groups and Social Categories*** — foundational social identity theory.

* **[M/E] John Turner et al., *Rediscovering the Social Group*** — self-categorization theory; essential for group identity and intragroup/intergroup dynamics.

* **[M/E] Richard Jenkins, *Social Identity*** — accessible sociological synthesis.

* **[M/E] Rogers Brubaker and Frederick Cooper, “Beyond ‘Identity’”** — article-level classic warning against sloppy uses of “identity.”

* **[M/E] Rogers Brubaker, *Ethnicity without Groups*** — important for avoiding reification of ethnic groups.

* **[M/I] Anthony D. Smith, *The Ethnic Origins of Nations*** — older but influential for ethnicity, nationhood, myth, and memory.

## Biblical and early Christian application

* **[BIB/E] J. Brian Tucker and Coleman A. Baker, eds., *T&T Clark Handbook to Social Identity in the New Testament*** — methodological and applied introduction to social identity in NT studies. Reviews describe the volume as bringing Social Identity Approach into one place for NT study, with methodological essays and concrete applications. ([The Gospel Coalition][3])

* **[BIB/E] J. Brian Tucker and Aaron Kuecker, eds., *T&T Clark Social Identity Commentary on the New Testament*** — comprehensive commentary organized around social identity formation in the NT; the series description emphasizes historically grounded and theoretically nuanced readings at the intersection of text and context. ([Bloomsbury][4])

* **[BIB/I] Philip F. Esler, *Community and Gospel in Luke-Acts*** — early and important use of social-scientific and identity approaches in Luke-Acts.

* **[BIB/I] Philip F. Esler, *Conflict and Identity in Romans*** — key application of social identity to Romans.

* **[BIB/I] Philip F. Esler, *Galatians*** — useful for ethnicity, law, group boundaries, and identity in Galatians.

* **[BIB/I] Andrew M. King, *Social Identity and the Book of Amos*** — one of the clearer Hebrew Bible applications of Social Identity Approach; it examines how Amos constructs what it means to be the people of God. ([Amazon][5])

* **[BIB/I] Brian Tucker, *Remain in Your Calling: Paul and the Continuation of Social Identities in 1 Corinthians*** — identity, ethnicity, and continuity in Pauline communities.

* **[BIB/I] Love L. Sechrest, *A Former Jew: Paul and the Dialectics of Race*** — race/ethnicity theory in Pauline identity.

* **[BIB/I] Denise Kimber Buell, *Making Christians: Clement of Alexandria and the Rhetoric of Legitimacy*** — early Christian identity, ethnicity, and formation; different from the title named earlier.

# 4. Narrative theory and narratology: general tools

These works help make narrative criticism more precise: narrator, focalization, time, plot, characterization, implied reader, narrative worlds, ideology, and reception.

* **[M/E] David Herman, ed., *The Cambridge Companion to Narrative*** — broad guide to current narrative study across literature, film, everyday storytelling, and other media. Cambridge describes it as an overview of current approaches to narrative analysis across diverse traditions and media. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])

* **[M/E] Danna Nolan Fewell, ed., *The Oxford Handbook of Biblical Narrative*** — the main biblical-narrative handbook. It has fifty-one chapters on biblical narratives, narrative criticism, socio-historical influences, genre, bodies, and narrative construction. ([OUP Academic][7])

* **[M/H] Gérard Genette, *Narrative Discourse*** — foundational for order, duration, frequency, voice, mood, and focalization.

* **[M/H] Seymour Chatman, *Story and Discourse*** — clear foundational distinction between story, discourse, narrator, implied author, character, and event.

* **[M/H] Wayne Booth, *The Rhetoric of Fiction*** — classic for implied author, unreliable narrator, and ethical/rhetorical effects of narrative.

* **[M/E] Shlomith Rimmon-Kenan, *Narrative Fiction: Contemporary Poetics*** — concise and durable narratological toolkit.

* **[M/E] Mieke Bal, *Narratology: Introduction to the Theory of Narrative*** — systematic and often used in biblical narrative studies; distinct from her book-specific biblical readings.

* **[M/I] Monika Fludernik, *Towards a Natural Narratology*** — important for cognitive/natural narratology and experientiality.

* **[M/I] David Herman, *Story Logic*** — narrative worlds and cognitive narratology.

* **[M/I] Peter Brooks, *Reading for the Plot*** — desire, plot, closure, and narrative drive.

* **[M/I] Frank Kermode, *The Sense of an Ending*** — apocalyptic, closure, and narrative temporality; especially useful for Daniel, Revelation, Gospel passion narratives, and reception.

* **[M/I] Paul Ricoeur, *Time and Narrative*** — deep philosophical account of narrative, temporality, emplotment, and history.

* **[M/I] Tzvetan Todorov, *The Poetics of Prose*** — structuralist narrative analysis; useful historically and methodologically.

* **[M/I] Dorrit Cohn, *Transparent Minds*** — representation of consciousness in narrative.

* **[M/I] H. Porter Abbott, *The Cambridge Introduction to Narrative*** — compact entry point.

* **[M/I] James Phelan, *Living to Tell about It*** — rhetorical narratology; useful for ethical and persuasive dimensions of narrative.

* **[M/I] Susan Lanser, *The Narrative Act*** — feminist narratology and narrative voice.

# 7. Social memory: general theory

Social memory is one of the key bridges between narrative, anthropology, historiography, reception history, liturgy, saints, and identity.

* **[M/H] Maurice Halbwachs, *On Collective Memory*** — foundational. Memory is socially framed, group-shaped, and reconstructed in the present.

* **[M/E] Jeffrey Olick, Vered Vinitzky-Seroussi, and Daniel Levy, eds., *The Collective Memory Reader*** — probably the best first reader in memory studies; OUP describes it as presenting, organizing, and evaluating past and contemporary work on collective memory. ([Oxford University Press][9])

* **[M/E] Anna Lisa Tota and Trever Hagen, eds., *Routledge International Handbook of Memory Studies*** — broad current reference for memory studies across sociology, anthropology, philosophy, biology, media, archives, literature, and history. ([Routledge][10])

* **[M/E] Endel Tulving and Fergus Craik, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Memory*** — cognitive-psychological memory control; useful when biblical memory studies makes claims about individual memory, eyewitness memory, reconstruction, or distortion. ([Barnes & Noble][11])

* **[M/H] Pierre Nora, *Les Lieux de mémoire*** — foundational for sites of memory: monuments, texts, rituals, places, calendars, and symbols.

* **[M/E] Paul Connerton, *How Societies Remember*** — essential for commemorative ceremonies, bodily practices, and habitual memory.

* **[M/I] Paul Connerton, *The Spirit of Mourning*** — memory, mourning, and modernity.

* **[M/E] Aleida Assmann, *Cultural Memory and Western Civilization*** — major theoretical work on canon/archive, storage/functional memory, trauma, and cultural transmission.

* **[M/E] Astrid Erll, *Memory in Culture*** — compact, methodologically clear introduction to cultural memory studies.

* **[M/E] Astrid Erll and Ansgar Nünning, eds., *Cultural Memory Studies: An International and Interdisciplinary Handbook*** — strong interdisciplinary mapping.

* **[M/E] James V. Wertsch, *Voices of Collective Remembering*** — narrative templates, schematic narrative patterns, and national memory.

* **[M/I] Barry Schwartz, *Abraham Lincoln and the Forge of National Memory*** — useful for how historical figures become symbolic memory sites.

* **[M/I] David Lowenthal, *The Past Is a Foreign Country*** — heritage, nostalgia, historical consciousness.

* **[M/I] Andreas Huyssen, *Present Pasts*** — memory, urban culture, modernity.

* **[M/I] Michael Rothberg, *Multidirectional Memory*** — trauma, Holocaust memory, colonial memory, and noncompetitive memory.

* **[M/I] Marianne Hirsch, *The Generation of Postmemory*** — second-generation memory, trauma transmission, image, and family narrative.

* **[M/I] Dominick LaCapra, *Writing History, Writing Trauma*** — historiography, trauma, and working-through.

* **[M/I] Cathy Caruth, *Unclaimed Experience*** — trauma theory; influential, though often critiqued.

* **[M/E] Susan Alcock, *Archaeologies of the Greek Past*** — memory, monuments, landscape, and Greek antiquity.

* **[M/E] Ruth Van Dyke and Susan Alcock, eds., *Archaeologies of Memory*** — major collection linking archaeology and memory.

* **[M/E] Andrew Jones, *Memory and Material Culture*** — material objects as memory media.

* **[M/I] Yannis Hamilakis, *The Nation and Its Ruins*** — archaeology, nationalism, memory, and material past.

* **[M/I] Yannis Hamilakis, *Archaeology and the Senses*** — sensory experience, embodied memory, and material engagement.

* **[M/I] Lynn Meskell, *Archaeologies of Social Life*** — materiality, memory, identity, and social life.
