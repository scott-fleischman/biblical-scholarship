# 1. Meta-method: how to compare religions without making bad comparisons

This is the first missing layer. If you compare biblical religion to Greek, Roman, Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Persian, Buddhist, Islamic, or Indigenous traditions, you need method. Otherwise the comparison easily becomes parallelomania, genealogy without evidence, or “all religions say the same thing.”

## Primary / classic theoretical sources

* **[P/H] Max Müller, comparative religion writings** — historically important for the older “science of religion.”
* **[P/H] Émile Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*** — religion as social classification, ritual, and collective representation.
* **[P/H] Max Weber, *Ancient Judaism*; *The Sociology of Religion*** — charisma, priesthood, prophecy, rationalization, law.
* **[P/H] Mircea Eliade, *The Sacred and the Profane*; *Patterns in Comparative Religion*** — historically influential, now heavily critiqued.
* **[P/H] Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger*; *Natural Symbols*** — purity, classification, body, social order.
* **[P/H] Clifford Geertz, “Religion as a Cultural System”** — religion as symbol system.
* **[P/H] Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process*** — liminality, communitas, ritual passage.
* **[P/H] Pierre Bourdieu, *Outline of a Theory of Practice*** — habitus, field, embodied practice.
* **[P/H] Michel Foucault, *The Hermeneutics of the Subject*** — askēsis, self-formation, truth, spiritual exercises.
* **[P/H] Talal Asad, *Genealogies of Religion*** — critique of “religion” as a modern category.

## Secondary / method

* **[E] Jonathan Z. Smith, *Map Is Not Territory*; *Imagining Religion*; *Drudgery Divine*** — essential for disciplined comparison, especially Judaism/Christianity/Greco-Roman religion.
* **[E] Bruce Lincoln, *Gods and Demons, Priests and Scholars*; *Discourse and the Construction of Society*** — ideology, myth, classification, ritual authority.
* **[E] Jörg Rüpke, *Religion: Antiquity and Its Legacy*; *Pantheon*** — helpful for ancient religion as practice, agency, and lived religion.
* **[E] Jan Assmann, *Moses the Egyptian*; *Cultural Memory and Early Civilization*** — memory, monotheism, Egypt, and transmission.
* **[E] Catherine Bell, *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice*** — still one of the best tools for avoiding simplistic ritual analysis.
* **[E] Robert Orsi, *Between Heaven and Earth*** — lived religion and presence.
* **[I] Brent Nongbri, *Before Religion*** — useful for ancient Christianity/Judaism and the category “religion.”
* **[I] Russell McCutcheon, *Manufacturing Religion*** — category critique.
* **[I] Tomoko Masuzawa, *The Invention of World Religions*** — modern construction of “world religions.”
* **[I] Saba Mahmood, *Politics of Piety*** — agency, discipline, embodiment, and devotion; especially useful for comparing ascetic/pious formation.
* **[I] Birgit Meyer, media/material religion studies** — mediation, presence, images, materiality.

# 2. Ancient education, paideia, grammar, rhetoric, libraries, and scribal formation

This layer is essential because biblical interpretation was done by people trained in **scribal schools, rhetorical schools, monasteries, yeshivot, madrasas, cathedral schools, and universities**.

## Why it matters

It explains how people learned to read: memorization, copying, grammar, rhetorical exercises, commentary, citation, florilegia, moral formation, and elite literacy. It also connects ancient Jewish scribes, Greco-Roman rhetoricians, Christian catechists, monks, rabbis, and Islamic scholars.

## Primary texts

* **[P] Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria*** — the largest ancient account of rhetorical education.
* **[P] Theon, Hermogenes, Aphthonius, Nicolaus, *Progymnasmata*** — rhetorical exercises: chreia, fable, narrative, encomium, refutation, thesis.
* **[P] Dionysius Thrax, *Art of Grammar*** — grammar as a scholarly discipline.
* **[P] Aelius Donatus and Priscian** — Latin grammar; crucial for medieval Bible reading.
* **[P] Libanius, school exercises and letters** — late antique rhetorical schooling.
* **[P] Isocrates, educational speeches**
* **[P] Plato, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias*, *Phaedrus***
* **[P] Aristotle, *Rhetoric* and *Poetics***
* **[P] Seneca, Epistles on moral education**
* **[P] Mishnah Avot and rabbinic education traditions**
* **[P] Monastic rules, catechetical lectures, and school texts**

## Secondary references

* **[E] Yun Lee Too, ed., *Education in Greek and Roman Antiquity*** — broad control on classical education.
* **[E] Teresa Morgan, *Literate Education in the Hellenistic and Roman Worlds*** — essential for actual school practice.
* **[E] Raffaella Cribiore, *Gymnastics of the Mind*** — Greek education in Hellenistic/Roman Egypt.
* **[E] Raffaella Cribiore, *The School of Libanius*** — late antique rhetorical schooling.
* **[E] Henri-Irénée Marrou, *Histoire de l’éducation dans l’Antiquité* / *A History of Education in Antiquity*** — historical classic; French original remains important.
* **[E] Judith Evans Grubbs and Tim Parkin, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Childhood and Education in the Classical World*** — current large reference for childhood, schooling, pedagogy, and education across Greek, Roman, eastern Mediterranean, and late antique contexts. OUP describes it as presenting current research across a wide range of topics on ancient childhood and education. ([OUP Academic][1])
* **[I] Stanley Bonner, *Education in Ancient Rome*** — still useful for Roman education.
* **[I] Robert Kaster, *Guardians of Language*** — grammarians and society in late antiquity.
