Below is a **conversion/reorientation bibliography**, organized by the **destination**: conversion **to** a faith, people, practice, philosophy, community, identity, interpretive method, or way of life.

I am using “conversion” broadly. It can mean formal religious affiliation, repentance, return, intensification, initiation, philosophical reorientation, vocational change, political awakening, deconversion, or movement into a new textual/scriptural world. This is consistent with recent conversion studies, which treat conversion as a complex social, psychological, cultural, ritual, and communal process rather than only an instantaneous private experience. The *Oxford Handbook of Religious Conversion* is the best current large reference because it surveys religious change globally and across religions and methods; Rambo’s *Understanding Religious Conversion* remains a standard process model; and Nock’s *Conversion* remains the classic ancient-world starting point, especially for Greco-Roman, Jewish, philosophical, and early Christian change. ([OUP Academic][1])

**Key**

**[P] Primary source** — conversion narrative, law, autobiography, sermon, ritual text, polemic, testimony.
**[S] Secondary scholarship** — historical, sociological, anthropological, theological, literary, or psychological study.
**[M] Method / theory** — useful for studying conversion itself.
**[H] Historical classic** — field-shaping but sometimes dated.
**[REC] Reception artifact** — a conversion text that became culturally influential.
**[NE] Non-English or original-language control.**

# 1. To repentance, return, teshuvah, metanoia, moral reorientation

This is conversion **within** a tradition: turning back to God, Torah, Christ, the covenant, virtue, or disciplined life.

## Primary sources

* **[P] Hebrew Bible:** Deuteronomy 30; 1 Kings 8; Isaiah 55; Jeremiah 3–4; Ezekiel 18 and 36; Hosea 14; Joel 2; Jonah; Psalms 32, 51, 130.
* **[P] Second Temple:** Sirach; Wisdom of Solomon; Tobit; Prayer of Manasseh; 1 Enoch; Jubilees; Qumran Hodayot and Community Rule.
* **[P] Rabbinic:** Mishnah Yoma 8; b. Yoma 86a–b; Avot; midrashic treatments of repentance; *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer*.
* **[P] Medieval Jewish:** Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Teshuvah*; Rabbeinu Yonah, *Shaʿarei Teshuvah*; Baḥya ibn Paquda, *Duties of the Heart*.
* **[P] New Testament:** Mark 1; Luke 15; Acts 2; Romans 6–8; 2 Corinthians 7; Hebrews 6; Revelation 2–3.
* **[P] Early Christian:** Shepherd of Hermas; Tertullian, *On Repentance*; Cyprian, penitential letters; Augustine, *Confessions*.
* **[P] Medieval Christian:** penitentials; Peter Damian; Bernard; Anselm’s prayers; *Cloud of Unknowing*; Catherine of Genoa.
* **[P] Early modern / modern:** Ignatius, *Spiritual Exercises*; Teresa, *Life*; Wesley’s journal; Jonathan Edwards, *Religious Affections*; Rav Kook, *Orot ha-Teshuvah*.

## Secondary scholarship

* **[S] David Lambert, *How Repentance Became Biblical*** — essential because it challenges assumptions that “repentance” is a stable biblical category.
* **[S] Gary Anderson, *Sin: A History*** — sin as burden, debt, and moral reality.
* **[S] Mark Boda, repentance studies** — biblical theology and prophetic return.
* **[S] Alan Cooper, Jewish penitential prayer studies.**
* **[S] Joseph Heinemann, *Prayer in the Talmud*.**
* **[S] Rob Meens, *Penance in Medieval Europe*.**
* **[S] Abigail Firey, penitential studies.**
* **[S] Thomas Tentler, *Sin and Confession on the Eve of the Reformation*.**
* **[S] Moshe Sluhovsky, *Believe Not Every Spirit*** — discernment, confession, and spiritual transformation.
* **[S] Jean Delumeau, *Le péché et la peur* [NE]** — French classic on sin, fear, confession, and Catholic culture.

**Why this matters:** many biblical and postbiblical “conversion” texts are not about changing religions; they are about return, moral reorientation, purification, covenant renewal, confession, and re-habituation.

# 2. To the God of Israel / Israelite covenant allegiance

This includes ancient “turning to YHWH,” incorporation into Israel’s covenant world, rejection of other gods, and allegiance to Israel’s God.

## Primary sources

* **[P] Genesis 12, 17; Exodus 12; Ruth; Joshua 2 and 24; 1 Kings 8; 2 Kings 5; Isaiah 2, 19, 56, 66; Zechariah 8 and 14.**
* **[P] Ruth** — the paradigmatic narrative of joining Israel: “your people, my people; your God, my God.”
* **[P] Naaman in 2 Kings 5** — allegiance to Israel’s God without full incorporation into Israel.
* **[P] Isaiah 56** — foreigner/eunuch and inclusion in temple prayer.
* **[P] Jonah** — Nineveh’s repentance and the boundary between Israelite election and divine mercy.
* **[P] Deuteronomic and prophetic anti-idolatry texts** — conversion as exclusive allegiance.
* **[P] Josephus, *Antiquities*; *Against Apion*** — retelling Israel’s history and gentile attraction to Jewish law.

## Secondary scholarship

* **[S] Shaye J. D. Cohen, *The Beginnings of Jewishness*** — foundational for when “Jewishness” becomes a category involving ethnicity, law, religion, and conversion; UC Press describes the book as addressing one of the most complex issues of late antiquity. ([University of California Press][2])
* **[S] Matthew Thiessen, *Contesting Conversion*** — essential for genealogy, circumcision, and whether ancient Judaism imagined conversion as fully possible for non-Abrahamic gentiles; OUP frames it as a wide-ranging study of Jewishness, circumcision, and conversion. ([Oxford University Press][3])
* **[S] Martin Goodman, *Mission and Conversion*** — major challenge to assumptions about Jewish proselytism and Christian mission in the Roman world; OUP describes it as an important examination of Christian mission against ancient Judaism and pagan culture. ([Oxford University Press][4])
* **[S] Louis Feldman, *Jew and Gentile in the Ancient World*** — very large survey of Jewish-gentile relations, attraction to Judaism, and attitudes from Alexander to Justinian.
* **[S] John Barclay, *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora*** — identity, assimilation, and diaspora Judaism.
* **[S] Terence Donaldson, *Judaism and the Gentiles*.**
* **[S] Paula Fredriksen, *Paul: The Pagans’ Apostle*** — Paul’s gentile mission as eschatological inclusion, not simple “conversion to Christianity.”
* **[S] Matthew Thiessen, *Paul and the Gentile Problem*.**
* **[S] David Novak, conversion and Jewish law/theology essays.**

**Why this matters:** ancient “conversion to Israel’s God” is not always identical with later rabbinic conversion to Judaism. It may mean worship, covenant allegiance, circumcision, residence, law observance, temple participation, or eschatological inclusion.

# 3. To Judaism / Torah / the Jewish people

This is conversion to Judaism as a legal, communal, ritual, and identity process: circumcision, immersion, sacrifice in antiquity, Torah observance, rabbinic authority, and later communal recognition.

## Primary sources

* **[P] Joseph and Aseneth** — conversion romance: food, repentance, angelic visitation, new identity.
* **[P] Philo, *Special Laws*, *On the Virtues*** — gentiles, law, and proselytes.
* **[P] Josephus, *Antiquities* 20** — Izates of Adiabene and Helena; circumcision and conversion.
* **[P] Matthew 23:15** — Christian polemical witness to proselyte-making.
* **[P] Acts 2, 6, 13** — proselytes and God-fearers in early Christian narrative.
* **[P] Rabbinic:** b. Yevamot 47a–b; t. Demai; tractate Gerim; Ruth Rabbah; Mekhilta and Sifre on ger / stranger.
* **[P] Medieval:** Maimonides’ responsa to Obadiah the proselyte; Judah Halevi’s *Kuzari*; conversion responsa; polemical texts involving converts.
* **[P] Early modern/modern:** memoirs of converts to Judaism; Reform, Conservative, Orthodox conversion procedures; modern batei din documents.

## Secondary scholarship

* **[S] Moshe Lavee, *The Rabbinic Conversion of Judaism*** — important for the Bavli’s distinct role in constructing rabbinic conversion; the National Library of Israel summary notes that it studies how the Babylonian Talmud challenged and extended the rabbinic model of conversion. ([National Library of Israel][5])
* **[S] Shaye Cohen, *The Beginnings of Jewishness*.**
* **[S] Matthew Thiessen, *Contesting Conversion*.**
* **[S] Martin Goodman, *Mission and Conversion*.**
* **[S] Louis Feldman, *Jew and Gentile in the Ancient World*.**
* **[S] Gary Porton, *The Stranger within Your Gates*.**
* **[S] Lawrence Schiffman, conversion and halakhah in early Judaism.**
* **[S] Christine Hayes, Jewish identity, gentile impurity, and legal status studies.**
* **[S] Natalie Dohrmann, rabbinic identity and imperial law studies.**
* **[S] Michal Bar-Asher Siegal, Jews/Christians/rabbinic identity studies.**
* **[S] Todd Berzon, *Classifying Christians*** — relevant for how Christians and Jews classify religious others in late antiquity.
* **[S] David Nirenberg, *Conversion, Sex, and Segregation*** — medieval Jewish-Christian-Muslim boundary-making.
* **[S] Paola Tartakoff, *Conversion, Circumcision, and Ritual Murder in Medieval Europe*.**
* **[S] Elisheva Baumgarten, gender, household, and conversion contexts in medieval Ashkenaz.**

**Key questions:** Is conversion a legal change, genealogical fiction, ritual rebirth, communal adoption, covenantal incorporation, or a contested identity? Does conversion erase prior kinship? Do converts become “like newborns”? How do gender, marriage, slavery, and empire reshape conversion?

# 5. To Jesus as Messiah / the early Jesus movement

This is not automatically “conversion from Judaism to Christianity.” In the first generation, it often means accepting Jesus as Messiah, joining an apocalyptic renewal movement, receiving baptism, and reinterpreting Israel’s scriptures.

## Primary sources

* **[P] John the Baptist traditions** — repentance, baptism, eschatological reorientation.
* **[P] Gospels:** call narratives, healings, exorcisms, discipleship, Zacchaeus, Samaritan woman, thief on the cross, Peter’s confession.
* **[P] Acts:** Pentecost; Ethiopian eunuch; Cornelius; Lydia; jailer; Paul.
* **[P] Paul:** Galatians 1; Philippians 3; Romans 6; 1 Thessalonians 1; 1 Corinthians 12.
* **[P] Hebrews** — turning toward Christ as priest, sacrifice, and covenant mediator.
* **[P] Revelation 2–3** — intra-Christian repentance and recommitment.

## Secondary scholarship

* **[S] Alan Segal, *Paul the Convert*** — Paul’s conversion/call through psychological, Jewish, and apocalyptic frameworks.
* **[S] Paula Fredriksen, *Paul: The Pagans’ Apostle*.**
* **[S] Beverly Gaventa, *From Darkness to Light*** — conversion in Paul and early Christianity.
* **[S] Krister Stendahl, Paul’s call rather than conversion model.**
* **[S] J. Louis Martyn, apocalyptic Paul studies.**
* **[S] John Barclay, gift/grace and Pauline reorientation.**
* **[S] Matthew Thiessen, gentiles and circumcision in early Christ-belief.**
* **[S] Caroline Johnson Hodge, gentile kinship and Abrahamic identity.**
* **[S] Terence Donaldson, *Paul and the Gentiles*.**

**Key distinction:** Paul is often best discussed as a **call** or **revelatory reorientation** inside Jewish apocalyptic expectation, not simply a modern-style religious conversion from “Judaism” to “Christianity.”

# 28. Highest-yield conversion reading path

For a focused research program, read in this order:

1. **Rambo, *Understanding Religious Conversion*** — process model.
2. **Rambo/Farhadian, *Oxford Handbook of Religious Conversion*** — global and methodological map.
3. **Nock, *Conversion*** — ancient world baseline.
4. **William James, *Varieties of Religious Experience*** — psychology and classic conversion experience.
5. **Buckser/Glazier, *Anthropology of Religious Conversion*** — ethnographic corrective.
6. **Lofland/Stark, “Becoming a World-Saver”** — network/social model.
7. **Snow/Machalek, “The Convert as a Social Type”** — identity and discourse model.
8. **Gooren, *Religious Conversion and Disaffiliation*** — conversion careers.
