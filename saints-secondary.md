Below is a **secondary-source map for holy/devoted lives**: scholarship on saints, sages, pietists, mystics, monks, nuns, martyrs, spiritual directors, Hasidic masters, Mussar teachers, Orthodox elders, Catholic saints, Protestant devotional writers, and modern witnesses of lived holiness.

I am prioritizing sources **not already listed** in the earlier bibliographies. A few major reference works are included because they are specifically necessary for this topic.

**Key**

**[E] Essential** — major starting point or field-shaping work.
**[I] Important** — specialized, tradition-specific, or influential.
**[H] Historical classic** — older, sometimes superseded, still necessary.
**[R] Reference/tool** — dictionary, encyclopedia, database, bibliographic control.
**[T] Textual/hagiographical tool** — catalogues, critical hagiography, manuscript tools.
**[M] Method** — how to study sanctity, hagiography, asceticism, mysticism.
**[J] Jewish.**
**[C] Christian.**
**[NE] Non-English.**
**[OR] Original-language control text; English translation may exist.**

# 1. Core reference works and research tools

These are the “where do I start?” tools for saints, spiritual writers, monastic movements, religious orders, cults, and hagiography.

* **[R/T/C/E] Société des Bollandistes: *Acta Sanctorum*, *Analecta Bollandiana*, BHL/BHG/BHO tools** — the foundational infrastructure for critical study of Christian saints and hagiography. The Bollandists describe their work as the origin of “critical hagiography,” combining history and philology; their manuscript database BHLms gathers Latin hagiographic manuscripts described in Bollandist catalogues. ([Société des Bollandistes][1])
  **Adds:** source control. This is where scholarship becomes exact about which vita, miracle collection, passio, translation narrative, or recension is being discussed.

* **[R/C/E] David Hugh Farmer, *The Oxford Dictionary of Saints*** — concise, dependable reference for saints’ lives, cults, feast days, patronages, and iconography. OUP describes the revised fifth edition as covering more than 1,700 entries. ([Oxford University Press][2])
  **Adds:** rapid orientation, especially for Western saints.

* **[R/C/NE] *Bibliotheca Sanctorum*, 13 vols. Italian** — major Italian Catholic saint-reference work. Use when Farmer is too brief or Western-English coverage is thin. A Syriaca.org citation records it as a 13-volume Roman publication of the Pontifical Lateran University’s Istituto Giovanni XXIII. ([Syriaca][3])
  **Adds:** broader Catholic reference coverage, especially useful for less-known saints.

* **[R/C/NE] *Dictionnaire de spiritualité ascétique et mystique: doctrine et histoire*, French, 17 vols. in 21** — the great French Catholic reference work for ascetic and mystical theology, spiritual authors, schools, practices, and technical vocabulary. Library records describe it as 17 volumes in 21; it ran through the twentieth century. ([search.library.nyu.edu][4])
  **Adds:** the best single reference for Catholic spiritual theology and historical spirituality.

* **[R/C/NE] *Dictionnaire d’histoire et de géographie ecclésiastiques* / DHGE, French** — massive ecclesiastical-history reference for people, places, dioceses, monasteries, orders, cults, and controversies. Brepols describes it as an unparalleled church-history resource with tens of thousands of entries by specialists. ([brepolis.net][5])
  **Adds:** geographical and institutional control for saints, religious orders, and local cults.

* **[R/C/NE] *Dizionario degli Istituti di Perfezione*, Italian** — essential for religious orders, congregations, founders, rules, charisms, and institutes of consecrated life. Contemporary review material notes the scale of the project and the quality of its bibliographies and statistical information. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])
  **Adds:** the best tool for the institutional side of holiness: orders, congregations, reforms, founders, and charisms.

* **[R/C/E] Bernice Kaczynski, ed., *The Oxford Handbook of Christian Monasticism*** — broad current reference across Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Protestant, Anglican, and new monastic traditions. OUP describes it as treating multiple strands of Christian monastic practice in forty-four essays. ([OUP Academic][7])
  **Adds:** cross-tradition monastic map.

* **[R/C/E] Alison Beach and Isabelle Cochelin, eds., *The Cambridge History of Medieval Monasticism in the Latin West*, 2 vols.** — major current reference for medieval Western monasticism. Cambridge frames it as comprehensive, from late antiquity to the end of the Middle Ages, with attention to European research often inaccessible to Anglophone readers. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][8])
  **Adds:** institutional and social history of monastic holiness.

* **[R/C/E] Cheslyn Jones, Geoffrey Wainwright, and Edward Yarnold, eds., *The Study of Spirituality*** — older but still useful ecumenical reference for Christian devotion. OUP describes it as written by Anglican, Roman Catholic, Free Church, and Orthodox contributors and focused on the nature and form of individual Christian devotion. ([Oxford University Press][9])
  **Adds:** broad Christian spirituality framework.

* **[R/C/E] Philip Sheldrake, ed., *The New Westminster Dictionary of Christian Spirituality*** — reference work with more than four hundred entries on Christian spirituality. Westminster John Knox describes it as replacing the earlier Westminster dictionary and covering all aspects of Christian spirituality. ([WJK Books - WJK Books][10])
  **Adds:** current terminology, movements, practices, and modern scholarship.

* **[R/J/E] Arthur Green, ed., *Jewish Spirituality I: From the Bible through the Middle Ages*; *Jewish Spirituality II: From the Sixteenth-Century Revival to the Present*** — important scholarly essays on Jewish devotional life, biblical spirituality, rabbinic piety, medieval pietism, Kabbalah, Hasidism, and modern Jewish spirituality. The first volume is described as a broad scholarly treatment of Jewish spiritual wisdom from biblical through medieval periods. ([Google Books][11])
  **Adds:** the closest Jewish analogue to Christian spirituality handbooks.

# 2. Method: hagiography, sanctity, asceticism, mysticism

These works teach how to study holy people without either flattening them into “pious fiction” or reading hagiography naively.

* **[M/C/H] Hippolyte Delehaye, *Les légendes hagiographiques* / *The Legends of the Saints*** — classic Bollandist method. Delehaye separates historical memory, legend formation, literary convention, cult, and pious invention. Fordham’s description notes his work in evaluating documentary sources for saints’ lives and cults. ([fordhampress.com][12])
  **Adds:** critical method for saints’ lives.

* **[M/C/H/NE] René Aigrain, *L’hagiographie: ses sources, ses méthodes, son histoire*** — French methodological classic on hagiographical sources, methods, and history. A contemporary review calls it both a first-rate documentary resource and a living lesson in sound hagiographical method. ([Persée][13])
  **Adds:** broad French method after Delehaye.

* **[M/C/E] Thomas J. Heffernan, *Sacred Biography: Saints and Their Biographers in the Middle Ages*** — moves beyond “is this historically true?” to ask how saints’ lives work as sacred biography, moral imitation, communal memory, and theological anthropology.
  **Adds:** literary and theological rehabilitation of medieval saints’ lives.

* **[M/C/E] Thomas Head, ed., *Medieval Hagiography: An Anthology*** — technically an anthology of primary texts, but the introductions are a major guide to how hagiography reflects ideals, practice, and community. Head’s introduction notes that hagiography tells us as much about authors and users—their ideals and practices—as about the saints themselves. ([Routledge Textbooks][14])
  **Adds:** accessible genre map.

* **[M/C/E] Peter Brown, “The Rise and Function of the Holy Man in Late Antiquity”; *Society and the Holy in Late Antiquity*** — field-shaping model of the holy person as patron, mediator, healer, judge, and social broker in late antiquity.
  **Adds:** the holy person as social institution.

* **[M/C/E] Peter Brown, *The Cult of the Saints*** — foundational for relics, tombs, patronage, and the social function of the saints in Latin Christianity. University of Chicago describes it as the classic account of how medieval people engaged with saints and their relics. ([University of Chicago Press][15])
  **Adds:** cult, relic, tomb, and community.

* **[M/C/E] Vincent Wimbush and Richard Valantasis, eds., *Asceticism*** — large comparative collection on ascetic practice across traditions; useful for not isolating Christian or Jewish discipline from wider patterns of bodily training. A catalogue summary describes it as a broad historical and comparative perspective on asceticism. ([eBay][16])
  **Adds:** asceticism as cross-cultural practice.

* **[M/C/E] Bernard McGinn, *The Presence of God: A History of Western Christian Mysticism*, multi-volume series** — the major modern history of Western Christian mysticism. Crossroad’s description of vol. 1 says it traces origins from Jewish apocalyptic, Greek contemplation, the NT, early Greek patristic thought, monastic practice, and Augustine. ([Crossroad][17])
  **Adds:** the standard historical spine for Western mysticism.

* **[M/C/E] Amy Hollywood and Patricia Beckman, eds., *The Cambridge Companion to Christian Mysticism*** — especially good for problematizing “mysticism” as an early modern category and asking how older terms like “mystical,” “contemplation,” “vision,” and “union” functioned. Cambridge describes it as an interdisciplinary guide, focused especially on the third through seventeenth centuries. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][18])
  **Adds:** contemporary method and critical vocabulary.

* **[M/C/E] Julia Lamm, ed., *The Wiley-Blackwell Companion to Christian Mysticism*** — broader chronological sweep, with essays on mystics, texts, and movements. Wiley describes it as a comprehensive account of Christian mysticism from its origins to the present. ([Wiley-VCH][19])
  **Adds:** broader handbook coverage than McGinn alone.

* **[M/C/E] Edward Howells and Mark McIntosh, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Mystical Theology*** — treats mystical theology as a theological phenomenon rather than merely psychology or anthropology. OUP describes it as a guide to the mystical element of Christianity. ([Oxford University Press][20])
  **Adds:** doctrine, theology, and contemplative practice together.

# 3. Jewish holy life: sages, pietists, Kabbalists, Hasidim, Mussar teachers

## Jewish spirituality, saintliness, and pietism generally

* **[J/E] Arthur Green, ed., *Jewish Spirituality I–II*** — first broad stop for Jewish spiritual practice across periods.
  **Adds:** integrated map of Jewish prayer, Torah, mitzvot, Kabbalah, Hasidism, and modern spirituality.

* **[J/E] Gershom Scholem, *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism*** — historical classic for Jewish mysticism and saintly charismatic figures.
  **Adds:** the modern scholarly framing of Kabbalah and Hasidism.

* **[J/E] Moshe Idel, *Kabbalah: New Perspectives*** — revises Scholem by emphasizing technique, practice, ecstatic Kabbalah, and multiple models of mystical experience.
  **Adds:** embodied and experiential practices, not just doctrine.

* **[J/E] Joseph Dan, *Jewish Mysticism*, multi-volume series** — expansive history from late antiquity through Hasidism.
  **Adds:** detailed periodization and movement-by-movement control.

* **[J/I] Elliot R. Wolfson, *Through a Speculum That Shines*; *Language, Eros, Being*** — advanced studies of mystical language, embodiment, vision, gender, and symbolism.
  **Adds:** high-level theoretical reading of Jewish mystical texts.

* **[J/I] Rachel Elior, *The Three Temples*; *The Mystical Origins of Hasidism*** — important, sometimes contested, work linking priestly, temple, Hekhalot, Kabbalistic, and Hasidic forms of holiness.
  **Adds:** temple-priestly and mystical continuity models.

* **[J/I/NE] Ephraim E. Urbach, Hebrew works on the Sages and rabbinic theology** — important for ideals of Torah, piety, humility, and divine service among the rabbis.
  **Adds:** Hebrew rabbinic-theological control.

* **[J/I] Jeffrey Rubenstein, *Talmudic Stories*** — not a spirituality manual, but excellent for sages as moral, pedagogical, and ascetic figures in narrative.
  **Adds:** how rabbinic stories construct holy teachers.

* **[J/I] Daniel Boyarin, *Carnal Israel* and rabbinic body studies** — useful for embodied Jewish holiness, sexuality, Torah, and the rejection of easy ascetic categories.
  **Adds:** Jewish sanctity without assuming Christian ascetic norms.

## Hasidism

* **[J/E] David Biale et al., *Hasidism: A New History*** — the current large-scale scholarly history. Princeton’s JSTOR page describes it as a “must-read” for understanding this influential modern Jewish movement. ([JSTOR][21])
  **Adds:** the current historical baseline for Hasidic holy life.

* **[J/H] Martin Buber, *Tales of the Hasidim* and Hasidism studies** — romantic and selective, but historically decisive in modern reception of Hasidic holiness.
  **Adds:** modern existential reception of the tzaddik and Hasidic tale.

* **[J/E] Moshe Rosman, *Founder of Hasidism: A Quest for the Historical Ba’al Shem Tov*** — essential for separating legend, archive, and historical reconstruction.
  **Adds:** historical control for the Baal Shem Tov.

* **[J/E] Immanuel Etkes, *The Besht: Magician, Mystic, and Leader*** — major modern biography of the Baal Shem Tov.
  **Adds:** charismatic leadership, magic, mysticism, and communal authority.

* **[J/E] Immanuel Etkes, *Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liady*** — essential for Chabad’s founder and intellectual Hasidism.
  **Adds:** Hasidic saint as teacher, organizer, theologian.

* **[J/E] Arthur Green, *Tormented Master: A Life of Rabbi Nahman of Bratslav*** — major spiritual biography of R. Nachman.
  **Adds:** religious genius, depression, storytelling, prayer, and charisma.

* **[J/I] Ada Rapoport-Albert, *Hasidism Reappraised* and studies on women and Hasidism** — necessary corrective to older male-master-centered accounts.
  **Adds:** gender, authority, and historical complexity.

* **[J/I] Rivka Schatz-Uffenheimer, *Hasidism as Mysticism*** — important older study of devekut, mystical inwardness, and Hasidic thought.
  **Adds:** theology of cleaving to God.

* **[J/I] Joseph Weiss, studies on Bratslav and early Hasidism** — historically important for R. Nachman and Hasidic spirituality.
  **Adds:** early scholarly depth on Bratslav.

* **[J/I] Nehemia Polen, *The Holy Fire*** — essential for the Piaseczno Rebbe and Hasidic teaching during the Holocaust.
  **Adds:** holiness under catastrophe.

* **[J/I] Shaul Magid, *Hasidism on the Margin*; *From Metaphysics to Midrash*** — important for modern and theological readings of Hasidism.
  **Adds:** Hasidic theology, antinomian edges, and modern reception.

* **[J/I] Ariel Evan Mayse, *Speaking Infinities*** — contemporary study of early Hasidic homiletics and mystical language.
  **Adds:** Hasidic Torah speech as spiritual practice.

* **[J/I] Marcin Wodziński, *Historical Atlas of Hasidism*; *Hasidism and Politics*** — essential social-geographic history.
  **Adds:** where Hasidic holy communities actually lived, moved, and organized.

* **[J/I] Glenn Dynner, *Men of Silk*** — social history of Hasidism and Polish Jewish society.
  **Adds:** Hasidism as lived social network, not only doctrine.

## Mussar and ethical formation

* **[J/E] Dov Katz, *The Musar Movement: Its History, Leading Personalities and Doctrines*** — the classic large narrative of Mussar; the National Library of Israel catalogue notes the English version was translated from the fifth Hebrew edition. ([National Library of Israel][22])
  **Adds:** the standard old-school movement history.

* **[J/E] Immanuel Etkes, *Rabbi Israel Salanter and the Mussar Movement*** — more critical modern scholarship on Salanter, pedagogy, self-scrutiny, and Lithuanian yeshiva life.
  **Adds:** Mussar as religious reform and educational discipline.

* **[J/I] Hillel Goldberg, *Israel Salanter: Text, Structure, Idea*** — close study of Salanter’s thought and influence.
  **Adds:** intellectual structure behind Mussar practice.

* **[J/I] Geoffrey Claussen, *Sharing the Burden: Rabbi Simḥah Zissel Ziv and the Path of Musar*** — major recent work on the Kelm school and moral formation.
  **Adds:** Mussar as relational ethics and disciplined community.

* **[J/I] Alan Morinis, *Everyday Holiness*** — modern popular-practical Mussar; less academic, but important for contemporary reception.
  **Adds:** current lay revival of Mussar practice.

# 4. Early Christian ascetics, martyrs, monks, bishops, holy women

## Desert, asceticism, and late antique holy people

* **[C/E] William Harmless, *Desert Christians*** — best accessible scholarly guide to Antony, Pachomius, Evagrius, the desert fathers and mothers, texts, practices, and modern misreadings.
  **Adds:** desert monasticism without romanticizing it.

* **[C/E] James Goehring, *Ascetics, Society, and the Desert*** — crucial for Egyptian monastic origins, social setting, and critique of simplistic desert narratives.
  **Adds:** monasticism as social history.

* **[C/E] Derwas Chitty, *The Desert a City*** — older but still valuable synthesis of Egyptian and Palestinian monasticism.
  **Adds:** classic map of eastern monastic networks.

* **[C/E] Samuel Rubenson, *The Letters of St. Antony*** — important for reconstructing Antony as a literate, theological ascetic, not only the figure of Athanasius’s *Life*.
  **Adds:** Antony beyond the hagiographic image.

* **[C/E] David Brakke, *Athanasius and the Politics of Asceticism*** — places Athanasius, Antony, virgins, monks, and ecclesiastical politics together.
  **Adds:** ascetic holiness as ecclesial strategy.

* **[C/E] Susanna Elm, *Virgins of God*** — essential for female asceticism in late antiquity, especially Cappadocia and Egypt.
  **Adds:** women’s ascetic authority.

* **[C/E] Elizabeth A. Clark, *Reading Renunciation*** — classic on asceticism, gender, Scripture, and elite Christian interpretation.
  **Adds:** renunciation as textual and social practice.

* **[C/E] Virginia Burrus, *The Sex Lives of Saints*** — critical theoretical work on gender, sanctity, desire, and hagiography.
  **Adds:** destabilizes sanitized saintly bodies.

* **[C/E] Patricia Cox Miller, *The Corporeal Imagination*** — important for bodies, relics, dreams, materiality, and sensory holiness.
  **Adds:** embodiment and imagination.

* **[C/E] Derek Krueger, *Writing and Holiness*** — hagiography, authorship, and sanctity in late antique Christianity.
  **Adds:** writing itself as sanctifying practice.

* **[C/E] Daniel Caner, *Wandering, Begging Monks*** — essential for Syriac/Egyptian/Greek ascetic mobility, suspicion, and regulation.
  **Adds:** unstable and itinerant forms of holiness.

* **[C/E] Claudia Rapp, *Holy Bishops in Late Antiquity*** — shows bishops as holy persons, not just administrators.
  **Adds:** episcopal sanctity, authority, and social mediation.

* **[C/I] Peter Brown, *The Body and Society*** — classic on sexual renunciation, body, and Christian asceticism.
  **Adds:** large cultural history of renunciation.

## Martyrdom

* **[C/E] Candida Moss, *Ancient Christian Martyrdom*** — important current re-evaluation of martyrdom traditions across regions.
  **Adds:** martyrdom as diverse local discourse, not one uniform phenomenon.

* **[C/I] W. H. C. Frend, *Martyrdom and Persecution in the Early Church*** — older synthesis, still important for the field’s genealogy.
  **Adds:** broad historical frame, though needs correction.

* **[C/I] Robin Darling Young, *In Procession before the World*** — martyrdom as public liturgy and performance.
  **Adds:** ritual and spectacle.

* **[C/I] Daniel Boyarin, *Dying for God*** — Jewish-Christian martyrdom and boundary formation.
  **Adds:** martyrdom as shared/contested Jewish-Christian practice.

# 5. Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, and Oriental Christian holy lives

## Syriac

* **[C/E] Sebastian Brock and Susan Ashbrook Harvey, trans./intro., *Holy Women of the Syrian Orient*** — primary texts with major scholarly introduction; UC Press describes it as fifteen Syriac hagiographies from the fourth to seventh centuries, including martyrs, saints’ lives, pious romances, and reminiscences. ([University of California Press][23])
  **Adds:** women’s sanctity in Syriac Christianity.

* **[C/E] Robert Murray, *Symbols of Church and Kingdom*** — classic for Syriac symbolic theology, biblical imagery, liturgy, and holiness.
  **Adds:** the symbolic world behind Syriac devotion.

* **[C/E] Susan Ashbrook Harvey, *Scenting Salvation*** — essential for sensory piety, hymnody, liturgy, and Ephrem.
  **Adds:** smell, sound, body, and salvation.

* **[C/I] Sidney Griffith, *Faith Adoring the Mystery*** — Ephrem, Syriac theology, and poetic devotion.
  **Adds:** Ephrem as theologian of worship.

* **[C/I] Adam Becker, *Fear of God and the Beginning of Wisdom*** — East-Syriac school culture and ascetic-intellectual formation.
  **Adds:** holy learning and scholastic asceticism.

* **[C/I] Jeanne-Nicole Mellon Saint-Laurent, *Missionary Stories and the Formation of the Syriac Churches*** — hagiography and ecclesial identity.
  **Adds:** missionary saints as church-forming narratives.

* **[C/I] Kyle Smith, *Constantine and the Captive Christians of Persia*** — Persian martyrs and imperial memory.
  **Adds:** martyrdom under Sasanian rule.

## Coptic and Egyptian

* **[C/E] Rebecca Krawiec, *Shenoute and the Women of the White Monastery*** — essential for Coptic monastic life, gender, authority, and discipline.
  **Adds:** Shenoute’s holiness as communal governance.

* **[C/E] Caroline Schroeder, *Monastic Bodies*** — Coptic monasticism, gender, and embodied discipline.
  **Adds:** ascetic bodies and communal identity.

* **[C/I] David Brakke, *Demons and the Making of the Monk*** — demonology, ascetic struggle, and monastic identity.
  **Adds:** spiritual warfare as monastic anthropology.

* **[C/I] Stephen Emmel, Shenoute studies** — necessary for serious work on Coptic monasticism and Shenoute’s corpus.
  **Adds:** philological and textual control.

* **[C/I] Bentley Layton, Coptic monastic and Pachomian studies** — important for Pachomian texts and Egyptian monastic organization.
  **Adds:** rules, community, and manuscript tradition.

## Armenian, Ethiopic, Georgian

* **[C/I] Robert W. Thomson, Armenian Christian historical and hagiographical studies** — important for Armenian saints, translators, martyrs, and ecclesial memory.
  **Adds:** Armenian sanctity and national-Christian identity.

* **[C/I] Michael E. Stone, Armenian apocrypha and hagiographical traditions** — important for biblical and saintly reception in Armenian tradition.
  **Adds:** apocrypha, saints, and scriptural imagination.

* **[C/I] Steven Kaplan, *The Monastic Holy Man and the Christianization of Early Solomonic Ethiopia*** — major study for Ethiopian holy persons and royal/monastic Christianization.
  **Adds:** Ethiopian sanctity as social and political force.

* **[C/I] Getatchew Haile, Ethiopic saints and hagiography studies** — crucial for primary texts and textual scholarship.
  **Adds:** philological access to Geʿez holy lives.

* **[C/I] Alessandro Bausi, Ethiopic hagiography and manuscript studies** — important for textual transmission and Ethiopian Christian literary culture.
  **Adds:** manuscript and edition control.

# 7. Western medieval monasticism, mendicants, holy women, and mystics

## Monastic life and spiritual culture

* **[C/E] Jean Leclercq, *The Love of Learning and the Desire for God*** — classic on monastic culture as grammar, lectio, prayer, and desire for God.
  **Adds:** monastic scholarship as devotion.

* **[C/E] C. H. Lawrence, *Medieval Monasticism*** — broad and reliable institutional history.
  **Adds:** where monastic saints fit into movements and reforms.

* **[C/E] Giles Constable, *The Reformation of the Twelfth Century*** — crucial for reform, new religious movements, canons, monks, hermits, and spiritual ideals.
  **Adds:** twelfth-century explosion of religious life.

* **[C/I] David Knowles, *The Monastic Order in England*** — older but essential for English monastic history.

* **[C/I] Adalbert de Vogüé, French studies on Benedict, monastic rules, and early monasticism** — important for rule traditions and Benedictine formation.

1. **Bollandist tools: BHL/BHG/BHO, *Analecta Bollandiana*, *Acta Sanctorum*** — source control.

6. **Green, ed., *Jewish Spirituality I–II*** — broad Jewish devotion.

17. **Harmless, Goehring, Brakke, Elm, Clark** — early Christian asceticism.
18. **Brock/Harvey, Murray, Harvey, Griffith** — Syriac holy life.
19. **Krawiec, Schroeder, Brakke** — Coptic and Egyptian monasticism.
20. **Meyendorff, Louth, Russell, Alfeyev, Larchet** — Orthodox theosis, hesychasm, and spiritual healing.
21. **Leclercq, Constable, Lawrence, Knowles** — Western monastic culture.

28. **Dana Robert, Andrew Walls, Sanneh, Bediako, Lian Xi** — global holy lives and world Christianity.
