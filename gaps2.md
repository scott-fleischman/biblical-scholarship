Below is a **methodological gap analysis** across the whole project so far. The main missed layer is not another corpus of texts, but **how scholars decide what a text, artifact, ritual, tradition, saint, community, manuscript, or historical claim is**.

A useful way to divide the gaps:

1. **Methods of establishing the object** — textual criticism, philology, codicology, material philology, fragmentology.
2. **Methods of interpreting the object** — hermeneutics, genre, intertextuality, rhetoric, narrative, semiotics, psychoanalysis.
3. **Methods of contextualizing the object** — social history, anthropology, archaeology, law, economics, empire, gender, ecology.
4. **Methods of reception and use** — performance, ritual, liturgy, memory, material religion, lived religion, digital reception.
5. **Methods of critique** — feminist, womanist, queer, postcolonial, decolonial, race, disability, animal, ecological, trauma.
6. **Methods of comparison** — comparative religion, comparative theology, scriptural reasoning, translation studies, interreligious reading.

# 1. General exegetical method handbooks

We have used many individual methods, but we did not sufficiently list **methodological handbooks that train the workflow**: text criticism, translation, morphology, syntax, form, genre, redaction, tradition, historical context, literary structure, reception, theology, and application.

## What this adds

This is the “how do I actually do an exegetical project?” shelf. It is especially useful for PhD students because it forces methodological sequencing and prevents mixing incompatible claims.

## Key works

### English

* **John Barton, *Reading the Old Testament: Method in Biblical Study*** — classic orientation to OT methods; particularly useful for understanding how historical-critical, literary, and theological readings relate.
* **John Barton, *The Nature of Biblical Criticism*** — conceptual defense and clarification of criticism.
* **Stanley Porter, ed., *Handbook to Exegesis of the New Testament*** — substantial guide to NT exegetical methods.
* **Craig Blomberg and Jennifer Foutz Markley, *A Handbook of New Testament Exegesis*** — concise workflow for textual criticism, translation, historical context, literary context, word study, grammar, interpretive problems, theology, and application.
* **J. W. Rogerson and Judith Lieu, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Biblical Studies*** — broad field map; OUP describes it as 45 articles offering critical examinations of the progress and direction of debates in a highly technical and diverse field. ([OUP Academic][1])
* **Steven McKenzie and Stephen Haynes, eds., *To Each Its Own Meaning*** — accessible but still useful survey of biblical criticisms.

### German / non-English

* **[NE] Uwe Becker, *Exegese des Alten Testaments: Ein Methoden- und Arbeitsbuch*** — one of the best German OT methods workbooks; it presents current standard methods for Old Testament exegesis. ([Amazon][2])
* **[NE] Manfred Oeming, *Biblische Hermeneutik*** — important German work on hermeneutics and method.
* **[NE] Odil Hannes Steck, *Exegese des Alten Testaments: Leitfaden der Methodik*** — older but influential German OT methods guide.
* **[NE] Thomas Söding and Christian Münch, *Kleine Methodenlehre zum Neuen Testament*** — German NT method guide covering text criticism, situation analysis, context analysis, form, genre, tradition, and redaction analysis. ([Content Select][3])
* **[NE] Martin Ebner and Bernhard Heininger, *Exegese des Neuen Testaments*** — German NT methods workbook.
* **[NE] Sönke Finnern and Jan Rüggemeier, *Methoden der neutestamentlichen Exegese*** — newer German method book that walks through synchronic and diachronic NT methods.
* **[NE] Horacio Simian-Yofre, ed., *Metodología del Antiguo Testamento*** — Spanish-language OT methodology collection.
* **[NE] Wilhelm Egger, *Metodologia del Nuovo Testamento*** — Italian NT method guide; important in Catholic exegetical training.

# 2. Philosophical hermeneutics

We have often named “methods,” but less often the philosophical problem underneath: **what is understanding?** What is the role of history, tradition, prejudice, suspicion, reader, community, and application?

## What this adds

Hermeneutics keeps exegesis from pretending to be a neutral extraction of data. It asks how meaning emerges between text, reader, history, language, tradition, and practice.

## Key works

### Foundational

* **Friedrich Schleiermacher, *Hermeneutics and Criticism*** — foundational modern hermeneutics: grammatical and psychological interpretation.
* **Wilhelm Dilthey, *Introduction to the Human Sciences*** — historicist hermeneutics and human sciences.
* **Martin Heidegger, *Being and Time*** — pre-understanding, interpretation, temporality; indirect but decisive.
* **[NE/OR] Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Wahrheit und Methode* / *Truth and Method*** — philosophical hermeneutics, tradition, prejudice, Wirkungsgeschichte.
* **[NE/OR] Emilio Betti, *Teoria generale della interpretazione*** — Italian hermeneutical theory; important counterpoint to Gadamer because Betti defends objectivist interpretation.
* **E. D. Hirsch, *Validity in Interpretation*** — authorial intention and determinacy; still important as a foil.
* **[NE/OR] Paul Ricoeur, *Le conflit des interprétations*, *Du texte à l’action*, *Temps et récit*** — text, symbol, suspicion, narrative, time, action.
* **[NE] Peter Szondi, *Einführung in die literarische Hermeneutik*** — German literary hermeneutics.
* **Jean Grondin, *Introduction to Philosophical Hermeneutics*** — accessible map of the tradition.

### Biblical / theological hermeneutics

* **Anthony Thiselton, *The Two Horizons*** — Gadamer, Wittgenstein, and NT hermeneutics.
* **Anthony Thiselton, *New Horizons in Hermeneutics*** — broad philosophical and biblical hermeneutics.
* **Werner Jeanrond, *Theological Hermeneutics***.
* **Sandra Schneiders, *The Revelatory Text*** — Catholic/feminist hermeneutics.
* **Gerhard Ebeling, *Word and Faith* essays** — hermeneutics in Protestant theology.
* **Rudolf Bultmann, hermeneutical essays** — demythologizing and existential interpretation.
* **[NE] Pierre Bühler and Clairette Karakash, French/German hermeneutical theology essays** — useful in continental theology.

# 3. Philology, textual editing, stemmatics, and “new philology”

We discussed textual criticism, but not enough **editorial theory**: What is a “text”? Is the goal to reconstruct an archetype, edit a living tradition, preserve variants, or map textual plurality?

## What this adds

This is crucial for Hebrew Bible textual plurality, Septuagint, NT manuscripts, rabbinic texts, patristic catenae, medieval mystical texts, hagiography, vernacular Bibles, and printed reception.

## Classical and genealogical textual criticism

* **Paul Maas, *Textual Criticism*** — concise Lachmannian classic.
* **[NE/OR] Giorgio Pasquali, *Storia della tradizione e critica del testo*** — indispensable Italian classic; emphasizes history of transmission, not mechanical stemmatics.
* **[NE/OR] Sebastiano Timpanaro, *La genesi del metodo del Lachmann*** — history and critique of Lachmann’s method.
* **M. L. West, *Textual Criticism and Editorial Technique*** — classic practical guide.
* **Paolo Trovato, *Everything You Always Wanted to Know about Lachmann’s Method*** — modern defense and explanation of genealogical textual criticism; its stated problem is reconstructing a lost text as closely as possible from later copies. ([Google Books][4])
* **David C. Greetham, *Textual Scholarship*** — broad English-language introduction to textual scholarship.

## New Philology and material philology

* **[NE/OR] Bernard Cerquiglini, *Éloge de la variante* / *In Praise of the Variant*** — foundational New Philology text; it criticizes modern positivist editorial habits and privileges manuscript variance. The New Philology movement is commonly associated with Cerquiglini’s 1989 work. ([University of Helsinki Wiki][5])
* **Stephen Nichols, “Philology in a Manuscript Culture”** — programmatic essay for New Philology.
* **Kevin Brownlee, Marina Brownlee, and Stephen Nichols, eds., *The New Medievalism***.
* **Keith Busby, ed., *Towards a Synthesis? Essays on the New Philology***.
* **Paul Zumthor, *Essai de poétique médiévale*** — medieval textuality, mouvance, performance.
* **[NE] Karl Stackmann, “Neue Philologie?”** — important German critique.
* **[NE] Joachim Bumke, *Der unfeste Text*** — German medieval textual variability.
* **Jerome McGann, *A Critique of Modern Textual Criticism*** — social-text theory.
* **D. F. McKenzie, *Bibliography and the Sociology of Texts*** — material texts as social forms.
* **Roger Chartier, *Forms and Meanings*** — book history, reading, textual transmission.

## Fragmentology and indirect transmission

* **Glenn Most, ed., *Collecting Fragments*** — how fragments are made, ordered, and interpreted.
* **Brill’s New Jacoby / Felix Jacoby, *Fragmente der griechischen Historiker*** — indispensable for fragmentary Greek historians.
* **[NE] Jacoby, *FGrHist*** — original German scholarly apparatus remains important.
* **Anthony Grafton, *Forgers and Critics*** — forgery and critical detection.
* **[NE] Wolfgang Speyer, *Die literarische Fälschung im heidnischen und christlichen Altertum*** — major German work on ancient literary forgery.
* **Irene Peirano, *The Rhetoric of the Roman Fake*** — pseudepigraphy, forgery, and literary play.
* **Bart Ehrman, *Forgery and Counterforgery*** — early Christian pseudepigraphy and polemic.
* **Eva Mroczek, *The Literary Imagination in Jewish Antiquity*** — challenges modern assumptions about “books,” attribution, and textuality in ancient Judaism.

The largest remaining methodological correction is this: **ancient texts are not only semantic containers**. They are also **performances, artifacts, variants, legal acts, ritual scripts, memory technologies, social weapons, gendered bodies, colonial objects, ecological imaginaries, sacred sounds, and community-forming practices**. A strong research design should name which of those dimensions it is studying and which method authorizes the claim.
