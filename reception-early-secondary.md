Below is a **secondary-source control bibliography** for **Jewish and Christian textual reception, interpretation, canon, manuscript culture, liturgy, polemic, and medieval biblical use up to c. 1500**.

This is the counterpart to the primary-source map: not “what to read from Augustine/Rashi/Ephrem/etc.” but **what modern scholarship explains those traditions, authors, periods, and textual phenomena**.

**Key**

**[E] Essential/current anchor** — still a major starting point.
**[I] Important** — major, specialized, or one influential school.
**[H] Historical classic** — field-shaping, sometimes dated, still necessary.
**[T] Technical/textual resource** — textual transmission, manuscript studies, versions, Masorah, lexica, apparatus, bibliography.
**[R] Reference infrastructure** — encyclopedias, handbooks, companions.
**[L] Liturgical reception** — Scripture in worship, prayer, lection, chant, calendar.
**[P] Polemical/interreligious reception** — Jewish-Christian, Christian-Muslim, intra-Christian, anti-heretical, anti-Jewish, anti-Karaite.
**[V] Vernacular/material/visual reception** — vernacular Bible, drama, image-text, manuscript layout, popular devotional reception.

# 1. First-order reference infrastructure

These are the works to keep closest at hand. They cover many periods and traditions rather than one author or one book.

* **[R/E] *Encyclopedia of the Bible and Its Reception* / EBR, De Gruyter-Brill** — the broadest reference project for biblical reception, covering Jewish, Christian, Islamic, artistic, literary, and cultural reception. Brill describes it as a comprehensive reference work devoted to the Bible and its reception. ([De Gruyter Brill][1])
* **[R/E] Magne Sæbø, ed., *Hebrew Bible / Old Testament: The History of Its Interpretation*** — major multi-volume history of HB/OT interpretation from antiquity through modernity; especially relevant are **vol. I/1, Antiquity**, **vol. I/2, Middle Ages**, and **vol. II, Renaissance to Enlightenment**. ([Bookshop.org][2])
* **[R/E] *The New Cambridge History of the Bible*, vol. 1: *From the Beginnings to 600*** — crucial for ancient textual formation, versions, canon, Jewish and Christian interpretation, and early reception. It includes coverage of the New Testament period to 600, Coptic, Syriac, Latin Bibles, “Gnostic” use of Scripture, councils, and popular/non-literary biblical culture. ([Barnes & Noble][3])
* **[R/E] *The New Cambridge History of the Bible*, vol. 2: *From 600 to 1450*** — the best single macro-resource for the medieval Bible across East and West. It treats the Bible from late antiquity to the Reformation and includes Hebrew, Greek Christian, Jewish Greek, Latin, Ethiopic, Arabic, Armenian, Georgian, and Slavonic traditions. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])
* **[R/E] *The Oxford Handbook of Early Christian Biblical Interpretation*, ed. Paul Blowers and Peter Martens** — best current one-volume handbook for early Christian reception; covers transmission, authority, exegetical genres, liturgical interpretation, figures, schools, and traditions. ([OUP Academic][5])
* **[R/E] *The Oxford Handbook of the Reception History of the Bible*, ed. Michael Lieb, Emma Mason, Jonathan Roberts** — reception-history theory plus case studies; especially useful for clarifying what reception history is and how it differs from historical criticism. ([OUP Academic][6])
* **[R/I] Emma England and William John Lyons, eds., *Reception History and Biblical Studies: Theory and Practice*** — good for theory and method; explicitly engages the status of reception history inside biblical studies. ([Bloomsbury Publishing][7])
* **[R/E] Alan J. Hauser and Duane F. Watson, eds., *A History of Biblical Interpretation*, vol. 1: *The Ancient Period*** — strong synthetic essays on ancient Jewish and Christian interpretation.
* **[R/E] Alan J. Hauser and Duane F. Watson, eds., *A History of Biblical Interpretation*, vol. 2: *The Medieval through the Reformation Periods*** — covers medieval Jewish and Christian interpretation through the Reformation era. The volume is explicitly organized around major interpreters, methods, and movements in Jewish and Christian communities. ([Google Books][8])
* **[R/E] *Textual History of the Bible*, Brill** — indispensable for textual history across Hebrew, Greek, Aramaic, Syriac, Latin, Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, Georgian, Christian Palestinian Aramaic, Slavonic, and Arabic witnesses. ([Barnes & Noble][9])
* **[R/E] *The Cambridge History of Judaism*, especially vols. 3–5** — core historical background for late Second Temple, rabbinic, and medieval Judaism. Vol. 4 covers the Late Roman-Rabbinic period from 70 to 640 CE. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][10])
* **[R/E] Shmuel Safrai et al., eds., *The Literature of the Sages*, Parts 1–2** — core reference for Mishnah, Tosefta, Talmud, midrash, targum, liturgy, mysticism, and inscriptions.
* **[R/E] Christine Hayes, ed., *The Literature of the Sages: A Re-Visioning*** — newer major reorientation of rabbinic literature in the *Compendia Rerum Iudaicarum ad Novum Testamentum* series. ([Bryn Mawr Classical Review][11])
* **[R/T] H. L. Strack and Günter Stemberger, *Introduction to the Talmud and Midrash*** — still the most compact one-volume orientation to rabbinic literature; revised from Strack by Stemberger. ([Christianbook][12])
* **[R/T] *Oxford Bibliographies* entries on early Christian interpretation, rabbinic literature, targum, medieval Jewish biblical interpretation, patristics, Syriac Christianity, etc.** — not a substitute for monographs, but useful for controlled bibliographic entry points.

# 2. General history of interpretation and reception-history method

These works explain **how to think about reception**, not just where to find it.

* **[H/E] Henri de Lubac, *Medieval Exegesis: The Four Senses of Scripture*** — classic, massive, and still unavoidable for medieval Christian hermeneutics. De Lubac’s work remains one of the most influential modern treatments of medieval exegesis and the fourfold sense of Scripture. ([Eerdmans Publishing Co][13])
* **[H/E] Beryl Smalley, *The Study of the Bible in the Middle Ages*** — foundational for medieval Latin biblical scholarship; especially strong on the rise of literal exegesis and school methods. Smalley’s work is widely recognized as having changed the study of medieval biblical exegesis. ([Oxford Medieval Studies][14])
* **[E] Frans van Liere, *An Introduction to the Medieval Bible*** — best compact introduction to the medieval Western Bible as text, canon, commentary, schoolbook, liturgical book, and cultural object. It frames the Middle Ages between Jerome’s Latin Bible and Gutenberg’s printed Bible. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][15])
* **[H/E] Robert Grant and David Tracy, *A Short History of the Interpretation of the Bible*** — older but still useful as a concise map of interpretive history.
* **[E] John Sawyer, *A Concise Dictionary of the Bible and Its Reception*** — useful quick reference, especially for students.
* **[E/I] Susan Gillingham, reception-history essays and Psalms reception work** — important for reception history as bridge between historical-critical and theological/cultural study.
* **[E/I] Ulrich Luz, reception-history essays and Gospel commentary methodology** — significant for Wirkungsgeschichte in New Testament study.
* **[E/I] Brennan Breed, *Nomadic Text*** — useful theoretical work on reception and textual afterlives.
* **[I] Hans Robert Jauss, reception theory essays** — not biblical studies proper, but foundational for reception theory.
* **[I] Wolfgang Iser, reader-response theory** — relevant when reception history intersects literary theory.
