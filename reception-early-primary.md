Below is a **primary-source map** for **Jewish and Christian textual reception before the Reformation**, roughly from the Second Temple period through c. 1500, with a few **threshold items** just after 1500 flagged separately because they close the medieval manuscript world and open the Reformation/print world.

Modern editions and databases are mentioned only as **access points to primary texts**, not as secondary scholarship.

**Key**

**[E] Essential** — first-order primary source for reception history.
**[I] Important** — significant, often tradition-specific or specialized.
**[H] Historically decisive** — changed later reception, even if not “normative” everywhere.
**[T] Textual witness** — manuscript, translation, recension, apparatus, codex, canon list, gloss, catena.
**[C] Canon / creed / council** — primary source defining authoritative boundaries or doctrine.
**[L] Liturgical reception** — lectionary, prayer, hymn, rite, festival, homily cycle.
**[P] Polemical reception** — Jewish-Christian, intra-Christian, anti-heretical, anti-Jewish, anti-rabbinic, anti-Karaite, etc.
**[V] Vernacular / popular reception** — paraphrase, drama, legendary expansion, visual-textual tradition.

# 1. What you are looking for in these sources

For reception history, the key question is not only “what did this author think?” but **what form of the biblical or Jewish/Christian text did this community inherit, translate, quote, perform, contest, and canonize?**

The main things to track are:

1. **Text form** — Did they read Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, Arabic, Slavonic, or a targumic Aramaic form?
2. **Canon boundaries** — Which books counted as Scripture, ecclesiastical reading, oral Torah, apostolic writing, disputed, apocryphal, heretical, or useful but noncanonical?
3. **Citation practice** — Does the author quote exactly, paraphrase, harmonize, conflate, allegorize, or cite from memory?
4. **Interpretive method** — Literal, peshat, derash, allegory, typology, moral reading, anagogy, legal exegesis, mystical exegesis, philological criticism.
5. **Institutional setting** — synagogue, church, monastery, school, imperial council, yeshiva, cathedral school, university, polemical disputation, liturgy.
6. **Reception through use** — lectionaries, sermons, hymns, liturgical calendars, canon law, halakhah, monastic rules, scholastic quaestiones.
7. **Transmission apparatus** — masorah, marginal glosses, scholia, catenae, chapter divisions, Eusebian canons, gospel harmonies, correctoria, concordances.
8. **Cross-religious contestation** — Christian readings of Hebrew Bible, Jewish replies to christological readings, Muslim critiques of biblical corruption, Christian Arabic apologetics.

# 2. Primary-source access infrastructure

These are not “works to interpret” in the same way as Augustine or Rashi, but they are the **containers** through which you will encounter the primary material.

## Christian primary-source corpora and editions

* **[T/E] Patrologia Graeca / Patrologia Latina** — Migne’s massive Greek and Latin patristic/medieval collections. Outdated as critical editions, but still unavoidable for locating many texts.
* **[T/E] Corpus Christianorum Series Latina / Continuatio Mediaevalis** — critical editions of Latin Christian texts from the first centuries through the Middle Ages. The Series Latina covers the first eight centuries, while the Continuatio Mediaevalis covers the Carolingian era to the end of the Middle Ages. ([corpuschristianorum][1])
* **[T/E] CSEL — Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum** — critical editions of Latin Fathers and late antique Latin Christian authors. ([CSEL][2])
* **[T/E] GCS — Die griechischen christlichen Schriftsteller** — major critical edition series for Greek Christian writers. ([De Gruyter Brill][3])
* **[T/E] Sources Chrétiennes** — critical editions with French translation of Christian texts in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Aramaic, Georgian, and other languages, covering roughly the first 1400 years of the church. ([Brepols][4])
* **[T/E] Patrologia Orientalis / CSCO — Corpus Scriptorum Christianorum Orientalium** — major access points for Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Arabic Christian, Ethiopic, and other eastern Christian texts. Patrologia Orientalis is specifically a collection of non-Latin Christian Eastern texts. ([Brepols][5])
* **[T/I] NPNF / ANF collections** — English translations of many early Christian texts, useful as first access, though not critical editions. CCEL’s Fathers collection includes the Ante-Nicene Fathers and Nicene/Post-Nicene Fathers, including Eusebius and council documents. ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library][6])
* **[T/C] Acta Conciliorum Oecumenicorum; Mansi, *Sacrorum Conciliorum nova et amplissima collectio*** — major primary-source collections for councils.
* **[T/C] *The Seven Ecumenical Councils* in NPNF 2.14** — convenient English access to Nicaea I through Nicaea II. ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library][7])

## Jewish primary-source corpora and access points

* **[T/E] Tanakh textual witnesses** — Dead Sea Scrolls biblical manuscripts, Samaritan Pentateuch, Masoretic codices, targumim, medieval Hebrew manuscripts, Cairo Geniza fragments.
* **[T/E] Mishnah, Tosefta, Jerusalem Talmud, Babylonian Talmud** — core rabbinic reception of Scripture through law, narrative, and debate. The Mishnah is the first major rabbinic work and is traditionally associated with compilation around 200 CE; the Talmudim preserve generations of rabbinic debate and biblical interpretation. ([Sefaria][8])
* **[T/E] Midrash collections** — halakhic and aggadic interpretation of Scripture. Sefaria summarizes midrash as Jewish literature that interprets and elaborates biblical texts, with rabbinic collections beginning from the third century. ([Sefaria][9])
* **[T/E] Targumim** — Aramaic translations/paraphrases of Scripture. Sefaria describes targum as Aramaic translation of Tanakh, orally transmitted and written between roughly the first and sixth centuries CE. ([Sefaria][10])
* **[T/E] Masorah and Masoretic codices** — Aleppo Codex, Leningrad Codex, Cairo Codex of the Prophets, Ben Asher/Ben Naphtali traditions, Masorah parva/magna.
* **[T/E] Mikraot Gedolot / Rabbinic Bible tradition** — printed threshold form of medieval Jewish biblical reception: biblical text, targum, Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Ramban, masorah, etc.
* **[T/E] Sefaria** — not a critical edition, but a broad digital access point for Tanakh, Talmud, Midrash, medieval commentaries, halakhic codes, and later Jewish texts. ([Sefaria][11])

# 3. Pre-rabbinic and Second Temple Jewish reception

These are primary sources for how Jewish Scripture was read before later rabbinic and Christian institutional forms solidified.

## Jewish Scripture, rewritten Scripture, and textual plurality

* **[T/E] Dead Sea Scrolls biblical manuscripts** — indispensable for pre-Masoretic textual plurality.
* **[E/T] 1QIsaᵃ / Great Isaiah Scroll** — major witness for Isaiah’s textual transmission and Second Temple scribal habits.
* **[E/T] Qumran Samuel, Jeremiah, Psalms, Deuteronomy, Exodus, and minor prophets scrolls** — crucial for understanding textual forms behind later MT/LXX/SP traditions.
* **[E/I] Samaritan Pentateuch** — not merely a sectarian curiosity; central to Pentateuch textual history.
* **[E/I] Greek Jewish Scripture / Septuagint** — originally Jewish translation and reception, later Christian Bible.
* **[T/I] Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion fragments** — Jewish Greek revisions, preserved largely through Christian transmission, especially Hexaplaric materials.

## Qumran and sectarian interpretation

* **[E] Community Rule / Serekh ha-Yaḥad** — sectarian use of Scripture, identity, purity, predestination.
* **[E] Damascus Document** — scripturalized halakhah, covenant identity, Teacher of Righteousness traditions.
* **[E] 4QMMT** — halakhic disagreement and scriptural authority.
* **[E] Pesharim: Pesher Habakkuk, Pesher Nahum, Pesher Isaiah, etc.** — explicit “this refers to…” scriptural interpretation.
* **[E] Temple Scroll** — rewritten Torah as authoritative legal-temple vision.
* **[E] Jubilees** — rewritten Genesis-Exodus; calendar, covenant, law, angelology.
* **[E] 1 Enoch traditions** — apocalyptic rereading of Genesis, watchers, judgment, heavenly tablets.
* **[I] Genesis Apocryphon** — Aramaic rewritten Genesis.
* **[I] Aramaic Levi Document** — priestly reception of patriarchal tradition.
* **[I] Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice** — liturgical/mystical reception of priestly and heavenly temple traditions.
* **[I] 4QInstruction** — wisdom, revelation, eschatology, and scriptural anthropology.

## Hellenistic Jewish reception

* **[E] Philo, *On the Creation*, *Allegorical Interpretation*, *Questions and Answers on Genesis and Exodus*, *Life of Moses*, *Special Laws*** — essential for allegorical, philosophical, and Mosaic reception in Greek-speaking Judaism.
* **[E] Josephus, *Antiquities*, *Jewish War*, *Against Apion*** — rewritten biblical history, canon-like claims, apologetic history, priestly historiography.
* **[E] Letter of Aristeas** — reception legend of the Septuagint and Alexandrian Jewish textual prestige.
* **[I] Wisdom of Solomon** — Hellenistic Jewish rereading of wisdom, exodus, idolatry, immortality.
* **[I] Sirach / Ben Sira and the Greek Prologue** — crucial for wisdom, scribal self-understanding, and early awareness of Law/Prophets/other writings.
* **[I] Psalms of Solomon** — messianic and political rereading of Scripture after Roman intervention.
* **[I] 2 Maccabees; 4 Maccabees** — martyrdom, resurrection, Torah fidelity, scripturalized suffering.
* **[I] Pseudo-Philo, *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum*** — rewritten biblical history, likely near the first century CE.

# 4. New Testament and earliest Christian reception of Jewish Scripture

Even if one is not doing New Testament studies directly, the NT is one of the earliest major corpora of Jewish scriptural reception.

* **[E] Matthew** — fulfillment formulae, genealogy, Moses typology, prophetic rereading.
* **[E] Luke-Acts** — Scripture as salvation-history; Psalms, Isaiah, Deuteronomy, Joel, Amos.
* **[E] John** — temple, Logos, Moses, signs, Passover, Scripture fulfilled.
* **[E] Romans and Galatians** — Abraham, Torah, promise, Gentile inclusion, Habakkuk, Leviticus, Deuteronomy.
* **[E] 1 Corinthians** — Adam/Christ, wilderness typology, Passover, Scripture and community discipline.
* **[E] Hebrews** — priesthood, tabernacle, sacrifice, Psalm 110, Jeremiah 31, heavenly sanctuary.
* **[E] Revelation** — dense reception of Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, Exodus, Zechariah, Psalms.
* **[I] James, 1 Peter, Jude, 2 Peter** — wisdom, diaspora, Enochic traditions, prophetic judgment.
* **[T/I] Gospel harmonization and textual apparatus: Eusebian canons, Ammonian sections, kephalaia/titloi** — reception through how Gospel manuscripts were navigated.

# 5. Apostolic Fathers and subapostolic Christian reception, c. 90–150

These are essential for the transition from apostolic writings to “Christian Scripture.”

* **[E] 1 Clement** — early Christian reception of LXX, apostolic authority, church order, examples from Scripture.
* **[E] Didache** — Gospel tradition, commandments, liturgy, Eucharistic prayer, church discipline.
* **[E] Ignatius of Antioch, letters** — emerging Christian identity, Gospel/Pauline echoes, bishop-centered reception.
* **[E] Polycarp, *Letter to the Philippians*** — early Pauline reception.
* **[I] Martyrdom of Polycarp** — martyrdom shaped by passion narrative and scriptural imitation.
* **[E/I] Epistle of Barnabas** — allegorical and anti-Jewish reception of Torah; extremely important for early Christian hermeneutics.
* **[I] 2 Clement** — early Christian homily using sayings of Jesus and Scripture.
* **[I] Shepherd of Hermas** — quasi-scriptural authority in some early communities; visions, repentance, church discipline.
* **[I/T] Papias fragments** — early traditions about Gospel origins and oral transmission, preserved by later authors.
* **[I] Epistle to Diognetus** — Christian identity, Jewish law, and philosophical apologetic reception.

# 6. Second-century apologetic, anti-heretical, and canon-forming sources

This is where Christian reception becomes self-conscious: rule of faith, fourfold Gospel, anti-Marcionite canon, Christian Old Testament.

* **[E/P] Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho*** — indispensable for Christian-Jewish scriptural argument, messianic prophecy, LXX authority.
* **[E] Justin, *First Apology* and *Second Apology*** — public scriptural apologetic.
* **[E/H] Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*** — fourfold Gospel, apostolic succession, rule of faith, anti-Gnostic scriptural interpretation.
* **[E] Irenaeus, *Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching*** — compact scriptural-theological summary.
* **[H/T] Tatian, *Diatessaron*** — Gospel harmony; one of the most important early reception events in Syriac and wider Christian tradition.
* **[I] Theophilus of Antioch, *To Autolycus*** — creation, chronology, Scripture as antiquity.
* **[I] Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*; *On the Resurrection*** — apologetic reception.
* **[I/L] Melito of Sardis, *On Pascha*** — typological Passover reception; also associated with an early Old Testament canon list.
* **[T/C] Muratorian Fragment** — early Latin canon list, crucial for NT reception history.
* **[P/H] Marcionite Evangelion and Apostolikon fragments** — mostly reconstructed from opponents; critical for canon, Luke, Paul, and Christian use of Jewish Scripture.
* **[P/I] Ptolemy’s Letter to Flora** — Valentinian interpretation of Mosaic law.
* **[P/I] Gospel of Truth, Gospel of Philip, Apocryphon of John, Tripartite Tractate** — Nag Hammadi and related texts as alternative Christian scriptural reception.

# 7. Greek Alexandrian reception

## Clement and Origen

* **[E] Clement of Alexandria, *Protrepticus*, *Paedagogus*, *Stromata*** — philosophical, moral, allegorical, and pedagogical reception of Scripture.
* **[E/T/H] Origen, *Hexapla*** — the single most important early Christian textual-critical project on the Hebrew Bible/Greek versions.
* **[E] Origen, *On First Principles*** — theological interpretation, allegory, multiple senses of Scripture.
* **[E] Origen, *Commentary on John*** — major early Christian commentary tradition.
* **[E] Origen, *Commentary on Matthew*** — Gospel interpretation and ecclesial reading.
* **[E] Origen, *Homilies on Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Joshua, Judges, Jeremiah, Luke*** — Scripture as preached text.
* **[E/P] Origen, *Against Celsus*** — Scripture, apologetics, Jewish/Greek critique, Christian intellectual defense.
* **[E/T] Origen, *Letter to Africanus*** — Susanna, Daniel, Hebrew/Greek textual issues.
* **[I/T] Origen’s scholia and fragments preserved in catenae** — important for lost exegetical work.

## Later Alexandrian and Egyptian Greek reception

* **[E/C] Athanasius, *Festal Letter 39*** — one of the most important Christian canon lists.
* **[E] Athanasius, *Against the Arians*** — Nicene reception of Proverbs, Psalms, John, Hebrews.
* **[I] Athanasius, *Life of Antony*** — Scripture as ascetic script.
* **[I] Didymus the Blind, commentaries on Psalms, Ecclesiastes, Zechariah, Genesis fragments** — Alexandrian exegesis after Origen.
* **[E] Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on John*** — central for Christological reading of John.
* **[E] Cyril, *Commentary on Luke*, *Glaphyra*, commentaries on prophets** — typology, Christology, anti-Nestorian reception.
* **[C/P] Cyril’s letters against Nestorius** — scriptural reception in conciliar Christology.

# 8. Antiochene, Cappadocian, and Byzantine Greek reception

## Antiochene and historical-literal exegesis

* **[H/I] Diodore of Tarsus, fragments** — early Antiochene historical-literal method.
* **[E/I] Theodore of Mopsuestia, commentaries on the Minor Prophets, Psalms fragments, John, Paul** — critical for Antiochene exegesis, canon debates, and later East Syriac reception.
* **[E] John Chrysostom, homilies on Genesis, Matthew, John, Acts, Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Hebrews** — probably the most important Greek homiletic reception of the Bible.
* **[P] John Chrysostom, *Against the Judaizers*** — major, troubling, historically important witness to Christian anti-Jewish scriptural polemic.
* **[E] Theodoret of Cyrus, *Questions on the Octateuch*, commentaries on Psalms, Prophets, Paul** — one of the most important late antique Greek exegetes.
* **[I] Theodoret, *Eranistes* and *Ecclesiastical History*** — Christological and historical reception of Scripture.

## Cappadocians

* **[E] Basil of Caesarea, *Hexaemeron*** — classic reception of Genesis creation.
* **[E] Basil, *On the Holy Spirit*** — biblical argument for pneumatology.
* **[E] Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses*** — mystical and moral ascent reading of Exodus.
* **[E] Gregory of Nyssa, *Homilies on the Song of Songs*** — major allegorical/mystical reception.
* **[I] Gregory of Nyssa, *On the Making of Man*** — Genesis anthropology.
* **[E] Gregory Nazianzen, *Theological Orations*** — scriptural grammar of Trinity.
* **[C/I] Gregory Nazianzen’s canon poem** — canon-list witness.

## Byzantine Greek reception

* **[E/C] Ecumenical council acts and definitions: Nicaea I, Constantinople I, Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople II–III, Nicaea II** — Scripture as doctrinal proof, canon, and conciliar interpretation. The NPNF council collection conveniently gathers the seven ecumenical councils from Nicaea I through Nicaea II. ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library][7])
* **[E] Maximus Confessor, *Ambigua*, *Questions to Thalassius*** — advanced allegorical, Christological, and ascetic reception.
* **[E] John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*** — biblical synthesis of Greek patristic doctrine.
* **[E/P] John of Damascus, *On the Divine Images*** — iconophile reception of Scripture and tradition.
* **[E] Andrew of Caesarea, *Commentary on Revelation*** — decisive for Byzantine reception of Revelation.
* **[I] Oecumenius, *Commentary on Revelation*** — earlier Greek Apocalypse commentary.
* **[I] Procopius of Gaza, catenae/commentaries** — important catena tradition.
* **[I] Theophylact of Ohrid, commentaries on Gospels and Epistles** — late Byzantine synthesis of earlier Greek exegesis.
* **[I] Euthymius Zigabenus, commentaries and *Panoplia Dogmatica*** — Byzantine compilation and polemical reception.
* **[T/I] Greek catenae on Psalms, Gospels, Paul, Catholic Epistles, Octateuch, Prophets** — indispensable for reception and for fragments of otherwise lost exegetes.
* **[T/I] Euthalian apparatus to Acts, Catholic Epistles, Paul** — chaptering, prologues, reading aids, and paratextual reception.
* **[T/I] Eusebian canon tables and Letter to Carpianus** — Gospel comparison as textual technology.

# 9. Latin African, Roman, and Western patristic reception

## Latin apologetic and anti-heretical reception

* **[E/P] Tertullian, *Against Marcion*** — essential for Christian Old Testament reception, Luke/Paul textual history, and anti-Marcionite canon.
* **[E/P] Tertullian, *Prescription against Heretics*** — apostolic authority and interpretive ownership.
* **[I] Tertullian, *On Baptism*, *On Prayer*, *Against Praxeas*** — sacramental and doctrinal scriptural reception.
* **[E] Cyprian, *Testimonia ad Quirinum*** — biblical proof-text collection; essential for Latin Christian scriptural use.
* **[E] Cyprian, *Letters*** — Scripture in episcopal discipline, martyrdom, unity, rebaptism controversy.
* **[I] Novatian, *On the Trinity*** — Latin doctrinal reception.
* **[E/T] Victorinus of Pettau, *Commentary on the Apocalypse*** — earliest surviving Latin commentary on Revelation.

## Jerome and the Latin Bible

* **[E/T/H] Jerome, Vulgate translations and revisions** — decisive for Western biblical reception.
* **[E/T] Jerome, biblical prefaces** — especially the “helmeted prologue” to Samuel-Kings; canon, Hebrew truth, apocrypha.
* **[E/T] Jerome, *Hebrew Questions on Genesis*** — direct engagement with Hebrew, Jewish interpretation, and textual variants.
* **[E] Jerome, commentaries on Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Minor Prophets, Matthew, Galatians, Ephesians, Titus, Philemon** — Latin reception using Hebrew, Greek, Origen, and rabbinic/Jewish informants.
* **[E/T] Jerome, Letter 57 to Pammachius, *On the Best Method of Translating*** — translation theory.
* **[I/P] Jerome, *Against Rufinus*** — Origenist controversy and reception of Greek exegesis.
* **[I] Jerome, *On Illustrious Men*** — early Christian literary memory and canon-adjacent traditions.

## Ambrose, Hilary, Augustine, Gregory

* **[E] Hilary of Poitiers, *On the Trinity*** — Latin Nicene scriptural argument.
* **[I] Hilary, *Commentary on Matthew*; *Tractates on the Psalms*** — early Latin exegesis.
* **[E] Ambrose, *Hexaemeron*** — Latin reception of Basil and Genesis.
* **[E] Ambrose, *Exposition of the Gospel of Luke*** — major Latin Gospel reception.
* **[I] Ambrose, *On the Mysteries*, *On the Sacraments*, *On Isaac*, *On Jacob*** — sacramental and allegorical Scripture.
* **[E] Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*** — probably the most important Latin primary source on biblical interpretation.
* **[E] Augustine, *Confessions*** — Scripture as conversion, memory, prayer, and philosophical text.
* **[E] Augustine, *City of God*** — biblical history, canon, Rome, two cities, Genesis, eschatology.
* **[E] Augustine, *Literal Meaning of Genesis*** — creation, literal reading, science, allegory.
* **[E] Augustine, *Expositions of the Psalms*** — massive ecclesial/Christological reception of Psalms.
* **[E] Augustine, *Tractates on John*; *Homilies on 1 John*** — Johannine reception.
* **[E] Augustine, *Harmony of the Gospels*** — Gospel textual and narrative reconciliation.
* **[I] Augustine, *Questions on the Heptateuch*** — textual and exegetical problems in Torah.
* **[I/P] Augustine, *Against Faustus*** — anti-Manichaean scriptural reception.
* **[I] Augustine, *Retractions*** — authorial reception of his own exegetical corpus.
* **[E] Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Job*** — one of the most influential medieval Latin biblical interpretations.
* **[E] Gregory, *Homilies on Ezekiel* and *Homilies on the Gospels*** — pastoral and allegorical reception.
* **[I] Gregory, *Pastoral Rule* and *Dialogues*** — scripturalized ecclesial authority and sanctity.

# 10. Canon lists, creeds, councils, and doctrinal reception

These texts matter because they define **what gets read, how, and under what doctrinal grammar**.

## Christian canon-list witnesses

* **[T/C/E] Muratorian Fragment** — early NT canon witness.
* **[T/C/E] Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25** — homologoumena, antilegomena, spurious writings.
* **[T/C/E] Athanasius, *Festal Letter 39*** — canonical, read, and apocryphal distinctions.
* **[T/C] Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lectures* 4** — catechetical canon list.
* **[T/C] Gregory Nazianzen, canon poem** — poetic canon list.
* **[T/C] Amphilochius of Iconium, *Iambics to Seleucus*** — canon list with disputed books.
* **[T/C] Council of Laodicea, canon list tradition** — important but textually debated.
* **[T/C] Rufinus, *Commentary on the Apostles’ Creed*** — Latin canon classification.
* **[T/C] Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* 2** — Latin African canon list.
* **[T/C] Councils of Hippo and Carthage** — African conciliar canon tradition.
* **[T/C] Decretum Gelasianum** — late antique/early medieval Latin canon and apocrypha list.

## Creeds and councils as scriptural reception

* **[C/E] Nicene Creed, canons of Nicaea I** — reception of John, Proverbs, Psalms, and baptismal confession in anti-Arian context.
* **[C/E] Constantinople I / Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed** — expanded Trinitarian reception.
* **[C/E] Ephesus acts and Cyril’s letters** — Mary/Theotokos and Christological reading.
* **[C/E] Chalcedonian Definition and Leo’s Tome** — scriptural reception in two-natures Christology.
* **[C/I] Constantinople II and III acts** — reception of earlier fathers and Christological texts.
* **[C/E] Nicaea II acts** — biblical and patristic reception in defense of icons.
* **[C/I] Quinisext / Trullo canons** — liturgical, canonical, and disciplinary reception.
* **[C/I] Lateran IV, Lyons II, Florence** — medieval doctrinal and ecclesial reception of Scripture, sacraments, Jews, Greeks, and ecclesial unity.

# 11. Syriac and East-Syriac / West-Syriac reception

Syriac is essential because it preserves an early Semitic Christian reception stream, often not simply derivative of Greek or Latin.

## Syriac Bible and textual witnesses

* **[T/E] Old Syriac Gospels: Curetonian and Sinaitic** — early Syriac Gospel textual witnesses.
* **[T/E] Peshitta Old and New Testaments** — central Syriac biblical text.
* **[T/H] Diatessaron tradition** — Gospel harmony, especially important in Syriac Christianity.
* **[T/I] Philoxenian and Harklean Syriac versions** — highly important for Greek-Syriac textual history.
* **[T/I] Christian Palestinian Aramaic versions** — important Christian Aramaic biblical witnesses.
* **[T/I] Syriac lectionaries and Gospel books** — liturgical reception.

## Syriac writers

* **[E] Aphrahat, *Demonstrations*** — Persian Christian reception of Scripture; covenant, circumcision, faith, Israel, asceticism.
* **[E] Ephrem, *Commentary on Genesis*, *Commentary on Exodus*, *Commentary on the Diatessaron*** — essential Syriac exegesis.
* **[E/L] Ephrem, *Hymns on Paradise*, *Hymns on Faith*, *Nisibene Hymns*** — poetic and liturgical Scripture.
* **[I] Book of Steps / Liber Graduum** — ascetic and communal biblical reception.
* **[I] Narsai, metrical homilies** — East Syriac liturgical and exegetical reception.
* **[I] Jacob of Serugh, mimre/homilies** — West Syriac poetic biblical reception.
* **[I/P] Philoxenus of Mabbug, letters and polemical writings** — miaphysite reception and Syriac Bible revision.
* **[E] Ishoʿdad of Merv, commentaries** — major East Syriac commentary tradition.
* **[I] Dionysius bar Salibi, commentaries** — West Syriac medieval exegesis.
* **[E/I] Bar Hebraeus, *Storehouse of Secrets*, *Candelabrum of the Sanctuary*** — Syriac encyclopedic and exegetical reception.
* **[I] Syriac Cave of Treasures** — rewritten biblical history, Adam-to-Christ typology.

# 12. Coptic, Egyptian monastic, and desert reception

# 28. The highest-yield primary-source reading shelf

If the goal is a **first-pass essential primary-source curriculum**, I would prioritize these.

## Jewish

1. **Dead Sea Scrolls biblical manuscripts and pesharim** — textual plurality and early interpretation.
2. **Jubilees and 1 Enoch** — rewritten Scripture and apocalyptic reception.
3. **Philo, *On the Creation*, *Allegorical Interpretation*, *Life of Moses*** — Greek Jewish allegorical/philosophical reception.
4. **Josephus, *Antiquities* and *Against Apion*** — rewritten Bible and canon-like claims.
5. **Targum Onkelos, Targum Jonathan, Targum Neofiti** — Aramaic interpretive translation.
6. **Mishnah, especially Avot, Yadayim, Megillah, Berakhot, Pesahim, Yoma** — rabbinic reception of Scripture and practice.
7. **Mekhilta, Sifra, Sifre Deuteronomy** — tannaitic scriptural exegesis.
8. **Genesis Rabbah, Leviticus Rabbah, Lamentations Rabbah, Song Rabbah** — classic aggadic reception.
9. **Babylonian Talmud, especially Berakhot, Megillah, Sanhedrin, Bava Batra, Menahot** — biblical interpretation embedded in legal discourse.
10. **Aleppo/Leningrad Masoretic tradition and Masorah** — reception as textual preservation.
11. **Saadia Gaon’s Tafsir and *Book of Beliefs and Opinions*** — Jewish Arabic reception.
12. **Rashi on Torah and Prophets** — central medieval Jewish commentary.
13. **Rashbam, Ibn Ezra, Radak** — peshat, grammar, and philological reception.
14. **Maimonides, *Guide* and *Mishneh Torah*** — philosophical/legal reception.
15. **Ramban on Torah** — peshat, midrash, law, kabbalah.
16. **Zohar** — medieval mystical reception.
17. **Passover Haggadah, Siddur Rav Amram, piyyutim** — liturgical reception.
18. **Nahmanides’ Barcelona Disputation, Sefer Nizzahon, Toledot Yeshu traditions** — Jewish anti-Christian reception.

## Christian Greek and Byzantine

1. **New Testament, especially Matthew, John, Romans, Galatians, Hebrews, Revelation** — earliest Christian reception of Jewish Scripture.
2. **1 Clement, Didache, Ignatius, Barnabas, Shepherd of Hermas** — subapostolic reception.
3. **Justin, *Dialogue with Trypho*** — foundational Christian-Jewish scriptural debate.
4. **Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*** — fourfold Gospel, canon, rule of faith.
5. **Origen, *Hexapla*, *On First Principles*, homilies/commentaries** — textual criticism and allegorical exegesis.
6. **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*** — canon categories and early Christian textual memory.
7. **Athanasius, *Festal Letter 39*, *Against the Arians*** — canon and Nicene reception.
8. **Basil, *Hexaemeron*; Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses*; Gregory Nazianzen, *Theological Orations*** — Cappadocian reception.
9. **John Chrysostom’s biblical homilies** — Greek preaching reception.
10. **Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on John* and letters against Nestorius** — Christological exegesis.
11. **Theodoret’s commentaries and questions** — Greek exegetical synthesis.
12. **Maximus Confessor, *Ambigua* and *Questions to Thalassius*** — advanced Byzantine reception.
13. **John of Damascus, *On the Divine Images* and *Exposition*** — icon and doctrinal reception.
14. **Andrew of Caesarea on Revelation** — Byzantine Apocalypse reception.
15. **Greek catenae and Eusebian apparatus** — paratextual reception.

## Christian Latin

1. **Tertullian, *Against Marcion*** — canon, Old Testament, Luke/Paul.
2. **Cyprian, *Testimonia*** — Latin proof-text reception.
3. **Victorinus of Pettau on Revelation** — early Latin Apocalypse.
4. **Jerome’s Vulgate, prologues, *Hebrew Questions on Genesis*, commentaries** — decisive Latin textual reception.
5. **Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*** — Latin hermeneutical foundation.
6. **Augustine, *City of God*, *Literal Meaning of Genesis*, *Expositions of Psalms*, *Tractates on John*** — massive Latin reception.
7. **Ambrose, *Hexaemeron* and *Exposition of Luke*** — Latin allegorical and liturgical reception.
8. **Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Job*** — medieval Latin reception engine.
9. **Bede’s commentaries** — early medieval biblical reception.
10. **Glossa Ordinaria** — central medieval Latin reception artifact.
11. **Peter Lombard, *Sentences* and *Magna Glossatura*** — scholastic organization of Scripture and Fathers.
12. **Hugh and Andrew of Saint Victor** — theory of reading and literal exegesis.
13. **Thomas Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* and biblical commentaries** — scholastic patristic synthesis.
14. **Nicholas of Lyra, *Postilla litteralis*** — late medieval literal exegesis and Hebrew learning.
15. **Ludolph of Saxony, *Vita Christi*; Pseudo-Bonaventure, *Meditations*** — late medieval devotional Gospel reception.

## Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, and other eastern traditions

1. **Peshitta and Old Syriac Gospels** — Syriac textual reception.
2. **Diatessaron and Ephrem’s commentary on it** — Gospel harmony tradition.
3. **Aphrahat, *Demonstrations*** — early Syriac Scripture.
4. **Ephrem’s Genesis/Exodus commentaries and hymns** — poetic and exegetical reception.
5. **Narsai and Jacob of Serugh** — metrical homiletic reception.
6. **Ishoʿdad of Merv and Bar Hebraeus** — medieval Syriac commentary.
7. **Coptic Sahidic/Bohairic Bible; Nag Hammadi codices** — Egyptian textual and alternative Christian reception.
8. **Shenoute’s sermons and letters** — Coptic monastic biblical reception.
9. **Armenian Bible and Koriun’s *Life of Mashtots*** — translation and national scriptural identity.
10. **Ethiopic Bible, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Kebra Nagast** — broad-canon and royal-theological reception.
11. **Old Church Slavonic Gospel codices and Gennadius Bible** — Slavic biblical reception.
12. **Christian Arabic Bible manuscripts and Abū Qurrah / ʿAmmār / Abū Rāʾiṭa** — Arabic Christian reception under Islam.
