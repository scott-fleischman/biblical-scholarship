At this stage, the remaining omissions are mostly **research infrastructure and boundary disciplines**, not whole new bodies of biblical literature. A final pass should add tools and categories that let a researcher **find scholarship, locate people and places, track images and objects, work with coins/seals/maps, evaluate provenance, and connect biblical/religious studies to broader humanities methods**.

# 1. Bibliographic control: the discovery layer

This is the largest practical gap. A specialist cannot rely only on hand-picked bibliographies; they need the major indexing systems.

* **L’Année philologique / APh** — essential for classical studies: Greek and Latin literature, history, archaeology, epigraphy, numismatics, papyrology, philosophy, religion, mythology, and early Christian/patristic material. The online database includes the annual index from vol. I in 1928 onward. ([Brepols][1])
* **Atla Religion Database** — broad religion/theology index for articles, reviews, and essays across religious studies and theology. Use it for biblical studies, church history, theology, ethics, mission, liturgy, and denominational scholarship. ([Atla][2])
* **Index Theologicus / IxTheo** — major open-access theology and religious-studies bibliography, especially strong for European-language scholarship and Bible-passage searching. ([IxTheo][3])
* **Index Religiosus** — especially important for theology, church history, and European-language religious scholarship; it builds on the bibliographies of *Revue d’histoire ecclésiastique* and *Ephemerides Theologicae Lovanienses*. ([Brepols][4])
* **International Medieval Bibliography / IMB** — the basic article index for medieval studies; it covers journals and miscellany volumes worldwide and is classified by date, subject, and location. ([Brepols][5])
* **RAMBI — Index of Articles on Jewish Studies** — indispensable for Jewish studies, Hebrew-language scholarship, Eretz Israel, and the State of Israel. The National Library of Israel describes it as a selective bibliography of academic articles across all fields of Jewish studies. ([National Library of Israel][6])
* **Index Islamicus** — essential for Islam, the Middle East, and Muslim societies from 1906 onward; it indexes monographs, articles, edited volumes, proceedings, and reviews in European languages. ([EBSCO][7])
* **Base d’Information Bibliographique en Patristique / BIBP** — useful for patristic bibliography, especially French and European patristics; the Sources Chrétiennes student guide points to it as a free patristic bibliographic resource. ([Sources Chrétiennes][8])
* **Old Testament Abstracts and New Testament Abstracts** — still useful for English-language biblical scholarship discovery, especially for older article-level research.
* **Religious and Theological Abstracts** — useful supplementary index, especially for theological and denominational literature.
* **Bibliography of British and Irish History** — useful for Bible reception in Britain, Reformation, English Bible, Puritanism, Anglicanism, and saints.
* **Iter Bibliography** — Renaissance, Reformation, early modern humanism, Christian Hebraism, print culture.
* **Feminae: Medieval Women and Gender Index** — useful for women saints, medieval piety, gender, monasticism, women’s writing.
* **Répertoire International de Littérature Musicale / RILM** — essential if tracking Psalms, chant, hymnody, or biblical music reception.

**Why this matters:** without these databases, a bibliography becomes Anglophone and monograph-heavy. Article literature in German, French, Italian, Spanish, Hebrew, Dutch, Polish, and Portuguese is where many specialized debates actually live.

# 2. Prosopography, onomastics, and “who is who?”

We covered saints and historical figures, but not enough **person-index infrastructure**. This matters for rabbis, bishops, monks, scribes, emperors, martyrs, donors, governors, priests, and named inscriptional figures.

* **Prosopographia Imperii Romani / PIR** — core reference for Roman imperial elites from Actium to Diocletian; the De Gruyter description states that it collects biographical data on the ruling classes of the Roman Empire and includes around 15,000 persons. ([De Gruyter Brill][9])
* **Prosopography of the Later Roman Empire / PLRE** — essential for late antique bishops, officials, aristocrats, emperors, and Christian/pagan elites, roughly third to seventh centuries.
* **Prosopography of the Byzantine Empire / PBE** — online prosopography for 641–867, covering the period when late antiquity was becoming medieval Byzantium. ([Prosopography of the Byzantine Empire][10])
* **Prosopography of the Byzantine World / PBW** — online prosopographical database for the Byzantine world, especially 1025–1180; useful for saints, clergy, aristocrats, seals, and imperial administration. ([Medieval Digital Resources][11])
* **Lexicon of Greek Personal Names / LGPN** — essential for Greek onomastics, inscriptions, papyri, and social history.
* **Trismegistos People** — especially useful for documentary papyri and non-royal individuals in Egypt between roughly 800 BCE and 800 CE. ([Trismegistos][12])
* **Trismegistos Places** — place-name attestations in papyri, inscriptions, and related ancient evidence. ([Trismegistos][13])
* **Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England / PASE** — useful for Bible reception, saints, monasteries, and ecclesiastical culture in early medieval England.
* **Prosopographie der mittelbyzantinischen Zeit / PMBZ** — German prosopographical control for middle Byzantine figures.
* **Prosopographisches Lexikon der Palaiologenzeit / PLP** — late Byzantine prosopography.
* **Onomasticon of the Hittite Pantheon and ANE personal-name corpora** — useful where names encode theology, ethnicity, language contact, or cult.

**Why this matters:** a great deal of reception history depends on networks: who knew whom, who copied what, who funded which church or synagogue, which bishop attended a council, which rabbi appears in which layer, which saint’s cult spread where.

# 3. Gazetteers, maps, geospatial modeling, and linked ancient places

We discussed geography, but not enough research infrastructure for **ancient place identification and spatial argument**.

* **Pleiades** — community-built gazetteer and graph of ancient places, publishing authoritative place information under open license for ancient-world research and computational reuse. ([Pleiades][14])
* **Barrington Atlas of the Greek and Roman World** — still the physical atlas standard for Greco-Roman geography; WorldCat summarizes it as 99 full-color maps covering the Greek and Roman world from the British Isles to India and North Africa. ([WorldCat][15])
* **ORBIS: Stanford Geospatial Network Model of the Roman World** — models Roman travel time and financial cost across roads, rivers, and sea routes. ([Orbis][16])
* **Pelagios Network** — linked open-data community and infrastructure for connecting heritage data, especially places, across online collections. ([Pelagios Network][17])
* **Ancient World Mapping Center** — cartographic and GIS resources for ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern history.
* **Digital Atlas of Roman and Medieval Civilizations / DARMC** — GIS layers for the Roman and medieval world.
* **Tabula Imperii Romani / TIR and Forma Orbis Romani** — older but important map/gazetteer projects.
* **The Princeton Encyclopedia of Classical Sites** — older but still useful for classical site identification.
* **Onomasticon of Eusebius / Jerome** — primary-source geography of biblical places; essential for late antique biblical topography.
* **Madaba Map studies** — crucial for Christian cartographic and pilgrimage reception.
* **Survey of Western Palestine maps and memoirs** — indispensable for nineteenth-century biblical geography and modern reception of the land.

**Why this matters:** “Galilee,” “Judea,” “Arabia,” “Babylon,” “the wilderness,” “Zion,” “Rome,” and “the diaspora” are spatial arguments, not neutral labels.

# 4. Iconography, visual indexing, and image-based reception

We discussed visual reception, but not enough **image-finding infrastructure**.

* **Index of Medieval Art, Princeton** — one of the largest and most important archives for medieval art, offering iconographic documentation in text and image from early Christian material to the mid-sixteenth century. Princeton made the online database free to users in 2023. ([Department of Art and Archaeology][18])
* **Iconclass** — major classification system for the subjects and contents of images, used by museums and libraries. The online browser includes the classification system, image data, bibliography, and links to related sources. ([Iconclass][19])
* **LIMC / *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*** and **WebLIMC** — core iconographic resource for Greek, Etruscan, and Roman mythology; WebLIMC derives from the large documentation assembled for LIMC and ThesCRA. ([WebLIMC][20])
* **Warburg Institute Iconographic Database** — art-historical image resource organized by subject/iconography, including religion, mythology, literature, history, daily life, science, allegory, and portraiture. ([Warburg Institute Iconographic Database][21])
* **Mandragore, BnF** — illuminated manuscripts and iconography.
* **Parker Library on the Web** — medieval manuscripts, including biblical, liturgical, and patristic material.
* **British Library Digitised Manuscripts / Catalogue of Illuminated Manuscripts** — biblical, liturgical, devotional, and hagiographical manuscript images.
* **Vatican Library Digital Collections** — crucial for biblical, patristic, Byzantine, Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, Arabic, and medieval reception.
* **Manuscripta Mediaevalia / Handschriftenportal** — German manuscript discovery.
* **e-codices** — Swiss manuscript platform.
* **Biblissima** — French manuscript and textual heritage portal.
* **Gallica** — BnF digitized manuscripts, early printed Bibles, patristic and theological works.

**Why this matters:** many biblical and saintly traditions were received visually before they were read privately: icons, mosaics, manuscript illumination, frescoes, reliquaries, pilgrimage badges, stained glass, printed broadsides, and children’s Bibles.

# 5. Numismatics and sigillography

Coins and seals are a major missing source class. They matter for imperial ideology, divine titles, iconography, rulers, cities, economies, Jewish revolts, Herodian politics, Byzantine Christianity, Islamic transitions, and saint/monastery administration.

* **OCRE / Online Coins of the Roman Empire** — records every published type of Roman imperial coinage from Augustus to Zeno and supports identification, cataloguing, and research. ([Ancient World Institute][22])
* **Roman Provincial Coinage Online / RPC** — authoritative account of Roman provincial coinage; the project aims to provide standard treatment of provincial coinage from 44 BCE to 296/7 CE, freely online. ([RPC][23])
* **Coin Hoards of the Roman Empire / CHRE** — database of Roman coin hoards; the public database currently contains millions of coins and thousands of hoards. ([Coin Hoards of the Roman Empire][24])
* **Nomisma.org** — linked open-data vocabulary and identifiers for numismatic concepts. ([Deutsches Archäologisches Institut][25])
* **Sylloge Nummorum Graecorum / SNG** — Greek coin corpus.
* **Roman Imperial Coinage / RIC** — printed standard corpus behind OCRE.
* **Meshorer, *Ancient Jewish Coinage*** — essential for Jewish, Hasmonean, Herodian, and revolt coinage.
* **Yaakov Meshorer, Gabriela Bijovsky, Wolfgang Fischer-Bossert, *Coins of the Holy Land*** — practical catalogue.
* **Alla Kushnir-Stein and Haim Gitler, Jewish and Levantine numismatics** — important for local civic and Jewish coinage.
* **Dumbarton Oaks Byzantine seals catalogue** — necessary for Byzantine prosopography, offices, monasteries, saints, bishops.
* **Prosopography and sigillography of Byzantium** — seals often preserve the only evidence for people, offices, churches, and devotional names.
* **Corpus of West Semitic Stamp Seals** — seals as names, iconography, administration, and religious-symbolic evidence.

**Why this matters:** coins and seals are miniature official theologies. They show what rulers, cities, bishops, donors, and communities wanted materially circulated.

# 6. Chronology, calendars, astronomy, and time-reckoning

We treated calendars only through Qumran/Jubilees. The larger field matters for priestly courses, festivals, Easter/Pascha, rabbinic calendar, Islamic calendar, Byzantine eras, regnal years, Seleucid era, dating formulas, and apocalyptic chronology.

* **Otto Neugebauer, *A History of Ancient Mathematical Astronomy*** — foundational for ancient astronomy.
* **Otto Neugebauer, *Ethiopic Astronomy and Computus*** — useful for Ethiopic Christian calendar/reception.
* **Sacha Stern, *Calendar and Community*** — essential for Jewish calendars and communal identity.
* **Sacha Stern, *Calendars in Antiquity*** — broad treatment of ancient calendars across the Mediterranean and Near East.
* **James VanderKam, calendrical studies on Jubilees and Qumran** — necessary for 364-day calendar debates.
* **Jonathan Ben-Dov, *Head of All Years*** — Qumran calendar, astronomy, and priestly time.
* **Roger Beckwith, *Calendar and Chronology, Jewish and Christian*** — older but useful for Jewish-Christian calendar issues.
* **Alden Mosshammer, *The Easter Computus and the Origins of the Christian Era*** — vital for Easter, computus, and Christian chronology.
* **Faith Wallis, *Bede: The Reckoning of Time*** — translation and commentary on Bede’s computistical text.
* **Denis Feeney, *Caesar’s Calendar*** — Roman time, calendar, and imperial power.
* **Jörg Rüpke, *The Roman Calendar from Numa to Constantine*** — Roman calendar as religious-political structure.
* **François de Blois and Islamic calendar studies** — Hijri chronology and late antique/early Islamic dating.
* **Jack Finegan, *Handbook of Biblical Chronology*** — practical reference, though not final for every problem.

**Why this matters:** many historical claims fail because dates, eras, regnal systems, lunar/solar calendars, intercalation, or festival cycles are misunderstood.

# 12. Pedagogy and curriculum: how to actually train

A final gap is not content but **sequence**. A field this large requires layered competence.

## Minimum research competencies

A serious researcher should be able to:

1. Search **Atla, IxTheo, RAMBI, APh, IMB, Index Religiosus, Index Islamicus**.
2. Identify a text in **Clavis Clavium, BHL/BHG/BHO, CPG/CPL, Pinakes, Syriaca, Beta maṣāḥəft**, or equivalent.
3. Locate places in **Pleiades, Barrington, ORBIS, Pelagios**, or regional gazetteers.
4. Use at least one iconographic system: **Index of Medieval Art, Iconclass, LIMC/WebLIMC, Warburg**.
5. Check coins/seals through **OCRE, RPC, CHRE, Nomisma, Dumbarton Oaks seals, Corpus of West Semitic Stamp Seals**.
6. Distinguish **critical edition, diplomatic edition, facsimile, translation, paraphrase, catena, florilegium, commentary, homily, lectionary, and anthology**.
7. Ask provenance questions before using “new” artifacts.
8. Read scholarship in at least **German and French**, with Hebrew, Italian, Spanish, Latin, Greek, Syriac, Arabic, or Coptic depending on specialization.
9. Understand how to move from **primary evidence → edition → interpretation → reception → modern scholarly debate**.
10. Keep a research log: databases searched, search terms, languages, editions used, manuscript witnesses, and excluded evidence.

# 14. The final answer

The major categories are now covered. What remained were the **control systems**:

* discovery tools,
* indexes,
* prosopographies,
* gazetteers,
* iconographic databases,
* numismatic/sigillographic corpora,
* manuscript portals,
* provenance ethics,
* calendars and chronology,
* documentary method,
* museum collections,
* environmental/demographic history,
* sensory and material practice,
* and research workflow.

The final practical rule: for any claim about a biblical text, saint, ritual, community, doctrine, manuscript, artifact, or reception tradition, ask:

**What is the primary evidence? Which edition or object record controls it? What language is the best scholarship in? Which database would reveal what I am missing? What non-textual evidence bears on it? What modern category am I imposing?**
