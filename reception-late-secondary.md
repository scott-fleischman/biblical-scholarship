Below is a secondary-source map for textual and reception history from the late medieval/Reformation threshold to the present, with emphasis on what was missing or underrepresented in the earlier lists.

I am using the same categories:

[E] Essential/current anchor — should be in a serious research library.
[I] Important — major, specialized, or tradition-specific.
[H] Historical classic — field-shaping, sometimes dated.
[T] Technical/tooling — editions, catalogues, textual history, bibliography, manuscript/print infrastructure.
[R] Reference infrastructure — handbooks, encyclopedias, large survey works.
[L] Liturgical reception — Bible in worship, prayer, lectionary, hymnody.
[P] Polemical/interreligious reception — Jewish-Christian, Christian-Muslim, anti-Jewish, anti-critical, apologetic.
[V] Vernacular/material/visual/popular reception — print culture, children’s Bibles, literature, art, film, digital Bible, Bible apps, public Bible.

The biggest gaps in the previous answers were: early modern book history; Reformation-era Bible beyond the Reformers’ theology; Catholic vernacular Bible and censorship; Christian Hebraism; early modern Jewish print; post-Reformation scholasticism; Bible societies; colonial/missionary translation; Haskalah and modern Jewish Bible culture; Bible in America; Bible and slavery/race; Bible and science; Bible and nationalism; global South / Asia / Africa / Oceania reception; digital and ethnographic Bible use.

1. Global reference and survey shelf, 1450–present

These are the high-level secondary works that keep the whole map from becoming fragmented.

* [R/E] Euan Cameron, ed., The New Cambridge History of the Bible, vol. 3: From 1450 to 1750 — the major macro-reference for the Bible from late medieval print and humanism through the Reformation and Enlightenment. It is especially important for original-language philology, vernacular translations, confessional Bible use, and the transition from manuscript to print. Cambridge’s summary emphasizes that this period saw renewed recovery of Scripture in original languages and definitive vernacular translations.  ￼
* [R/E] John Riches, ed., The New Cambridge History of the Bible, vol. 4: From 1750 to the Present — the indispensable macro-reference for the modern period: production/distribution, new critical methods, global reception, confessional reception, literature, art, film, science, and public culture.  ￼
* [R/H] S. L. Greenslade, ed., The Cambridge History of the Bible, vol. 3: The West from the Reformation to the Present Day — older than the New Cambridge replacement, but still useful as a history-of-the-field artifact. It covers the Bible’s effects in the West from the Reformation to the New English Bible.  ￼
* [R/E] Bruce Gordon and Matthew McLean, eds., The Oxford Handbook of the Bible and the Reformation — now a key current reference for canon, translation, print, Reformation hermeneutics, commentators, texts, and theological themes. It is recent enough that it fills a gap left by older Reformation histories.  ￼
* [R/E] Kevin Killeen, Helen Smith, and Rachel Judith Willie, eds., The Oxford Handbook of the Bible in Early Modern England, c. 1530–1700 — essential for English-language reception, because it treats the Bible as scholarship, politics, domestic devotion, literature, song, image, cheap print, and everyday idiom.  ￼
* [R/E] Michael Lieb, Emma Mason, and Jonathan Roberts, eds., The Oxford Handbook of the Reception History of the Bible — already relevant before, but here it matters as a methodological control: reception history studies the Bible’s afterlives rather than trying only to recover original meaning.  ￼
* [R/E] Henning Graf Reventlow, History of Biblical Interpretation, especially vol. 4: From the Enlightenment to the Twentieth Century — the best broad history of modern biblical interpretation as intellectual history. Volume 4 covers Enlightenment through twentieth-century criticism.  ￼
* [R/E] William Baird, History of New Testament Research, 3 vols. — essential for the history of NT scholarship from deism through the late twentieth century; vol. 3 covers mid- and late-twentieth-century methodological change.  ￼
* [R/I] Donald K. McKim, ed., Historical Handbook of Major Biblical Interpreters — useful prosopographical reference for interpreters from the Fathers through modern scholarship.
* [R/I] William Yarchin, History of Biblical Interpretation: A Reader — useful sourcebook with framing commentary; especially helpful for teaching the arc from premodern to modern reading.
* [R/I] John Sandys-Wunsch, What Have They Done to the Bible? A History of Modern Biblical Interpretation — accessible but serious account of modern critical interpretation.
* [R/I] Stephen Neill and N. T. Wright, The Interpretation of the New Testament, 1861–1986 — still a compact map of modern NT research.
* [R/I] Werner Georg Kümmel, The New Testament: The History of the Investigation of Its Problems — older but still useful for the genealogy of NT critical questions.

2. Late medieval to early print: vernacular Bible, Wycliffites, manuscript-to-print transition

This is a major gap because Reformation Bible reception did not begin in 1517. Late medieval lay Bible access, lection, glossing, preaching, devotional reading, and vernacular circulation are essential preconditions.

* [E] Eyal Poleg, Approaching the Bible in Medieval England — crucial for the Bible as material object, liturgical text, devotional artifact, and visual/textual object in late medieval England.
* [E] Eyal Poleg and Laura Light, eds., Form and Function in the Late Medieval Bible — important for the physical shape of late medieval Bibles: layout, format, glossing, portability, use.
* [E] Kantik Ghosh, The Wycliffite Heresy: Authority and the Interpretation of Texts — essential for authority, textuality, and interpretation in Wycliffite controversy.
* [H/E] Anne Hudson, The Premature Reformation — classic on Wycliffite/Lollard biblical culture and reformist reading.
* [I] Richard Rex, The Lollards — compact historical orientation.
* [I] Margaret Aston, Lollards and Reformers — important collection for late medieval religious reform and biblical critique.
* [I] Margaret Aston, Faith and Fire: Popular and Unpopular Religion, 1350–1600 — useful for image, text, devotion, and reform.
* [E/I] Ian Christopher Levy, Holy Scripture and the Quest for Authority at the End of the Middle Ages — crucial for late medieval authority debates around Scripture.
* [I] Mary Dove, The First English Bible — Wycliffite Bible textual and reception history.
* [I] Elizabeth Solopova, The Wycliffite Bible: Origin, History and Interpretation — more recent treatment of the Wycliffite Bible tradition.
* [I] Sabrina Corbellini, Margriet Hoogvliet, and Bart Ramakers, eds., Discovering the Riches of the Word — lay religious reading in late medieval and early modern Europe.
* [I] Alastair Minnis, Translations of Authority in Medieval English Literature — authority, translation, and interpretive legitimacy.
* [T/I] Christopher de Hamel, Glossed Books of the Bible and the Origins of the Paris Booktrade — indispensable for glossed Bible book history.
* [T/I] Laura Light, studies on the Paris Bible and thirteenth-century Bible production — necessary for standardized portable Bible culture before print.

3. Renaissance humanism, philology, polyglots, and “the Bible as ancient text”

This is the key transition from medieval ecclesial Bible to early modern philological Bible.

* [E] Jerry H. Bentley, Humanists and Holy Writ — classic treatment of Renaissance humanism and biblical scholarship.
* [E] Anthony Grafton, Defenders of the Text — not only biblical, but essential for humanist philology, emendation, and scholarly self-fashioning.
* [I] Anthony Grafton, New Worlds, Ancient Texts — Renaissance recovery of antiquity and the destabilization of inherited textual worlds.
* [E] Erika Rummel, Erasmus’ Annotations on the New Testament — essential for Erasmus’s NT philology and reception.
* [I] Erika Rummel, The Humanist-Scholastic Debate in the Renaissance and Reformation — context for the conflict between humanist philology and scholastic theology.
* [I] H. J. de Jonge, Erasmus studies on the Greek New Testament and humanist biblical scholarship.
* [I] Jan Krans, Beyond What Is Written: Erasmus and Beza as Conjectural Critics of the New Testament — important for early modern NT textual criticism.
* [E] Stephen G. Burnett, Christian Hebraism in the Reformation Era, 1500–1660 — central for Christian use of Hebrew, rabbinic learning, Hebrew grammars, and transmission of Jewish learning; Brill frames it as the rise of Christian Hebrew scholarship as an academic discipline across Catholic and Protestant settings.  ￼
* [H/E] Jerome Friedman, The Most Ancient Testimony: Sixteenth-Century Christian-Hebraica in the Age of Renaissance Nostalgia — older but important for Christian Hebraism and the appeal to Jewish antiquity.
* [E/I] Kenneth Austin, The Jews and the Reformation — important for how Jewish presence and Jewish textual authority mattered to Reformation-era Christian debates.
* [I] Allison Coudert, The Impact of the Kabbalah in the Seventeenth Century — essential for Christian kabbalah and early modern esoteric biblical reception.

4. Reformation Bible and Reformation hermeneutics

This section is not “Reformation theology generally,” but works that specifically help explain Scripture, translation, canon, exegesis, and interpretive authority.

* [E] Jaroslav Pelikan, The Reformation of the Bible / The Bible of the Reformation — concise but high-yield on philology, vernacular translation, canon, and visual/print Bible culture.

5. Catholic Bible, Counter-Reformation, censorship, and post-Tridentine reception

This was underrepresented before. Catholic Bible reception after Trent is not only “Vulgate conservatism”; it includes censorship, vernacular restriction, missionary translation, Jesuit scholarship, commentary, liturgy, and anti-Protestant polemic.

* [E] Gigliola Fragnito, The Bible and the Censorship of Vernacular Translations in Early Modern Italy — essential for Catholic censorship, vernacular Bible control, and lay access.
* [I] Gigliola Fragnito, Proibito capire — Italian-language landmark on church control of biblical understanding.
* [E] Wim François and August den Hollander, eds., Infant Milk or Hardy Nourishment? The Bible for Lay People and Theologians in the Early Modern Period — important for Catholic and Protestant vernacular Bible debates.
* [E] John W. O’Malley, Trent: What Happened at the Council — best compact guide to Trent; useful for Scripture/tradition/canon debates.
* [I] John W. O’Malley, Trent and All That — historiography of Catholic reform and Counter-Reformation categories.
* [I] R. Po-chia Hsia, The World of Catholic Renewal, 1540–1770 — broader Catholic renewal context.
* [I] Carlos M. N. Eire, War Against the Idols — image, iconoclasm, Catholic/Protestant visual reception.
* [I] Carlos M. N. Eire, Reformations: The Early Modern World, 1450–1650 — major synthetic frame.
* [I] Robert Bireley, Jesuit and Counter-Reformation studies — for political and scholarly Catholic reception.
* [I] François Laplanche, La Bible en France entre mythe et critique — important for French Catholic/critical Bible reception.
* [I] Pierre Gibert, histories of Catholic biblical interpretation and criticism.
* [I] Scott Mandelbrote, early modern Catholic/Protestant textual scholarship essays.

6. English Bible and early modern English culture

The English Bible deserves its own shelf because it is not just translation history; it is literary, political, domestic, polemical, typographic, and colonial history.

* [E] David Daniell, The Bible in English: Its History and Influence — massive history of English Bible translation and influence; useful but should be checked against more specialized scholarship. It is an 899-page Yale history of the English Bible and its influence.  ￼
* [E] David Norton, A History of the Bible as Literature — indispensable for the English Bible as literary artifact; Cambridge notes Norton’s argument that the KJV’s literary reputation developed only over time, not immediately.  ￼
