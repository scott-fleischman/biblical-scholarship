Below is a **gap audit** across the entire bibliography we have built so far. The main thing missing was not “more commentaries.” It was a set of **adjacent scholarly worlds**: manuscript cataloguing, hagiography, liturgy, preaching, education, ordinary devotional practice, non-literary evidence, Samaritanism, vernacular traditions, religious orders, pilgrimage, relics, healing, and local saint veneration.

# 1. Research infrastructure we underused: catalogues, claves, manuscript portals

This is the biggest technical gap. Commentaries and monographs are not enough; for reception history and holy people, you need **repertories of texts, manuscripts, saints’ lives, sermons, miracle collections, lectionaries, and apocrypha**.

## Late antique, medieval, and hagiographical textual tools

* **[E/T] Clavis Clavium / ClaCla** — open-access gateway to late antique and medieval Christian literature. It incorporates the major “claves” for Latin Fathers, Greek Fathers, Latin hagiography, Greek hagiography, OT apocrypha, and NT apocrypha. Essential before you start looking for editions or manuscripts of a patristic, hagiographic, or apocryphal text. ([About Brepolis][1])

* **[E/T] Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina / BHL; Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca / BHG; Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis / BHO** — the three classic catalogues of Latin, Greek, and Oriental Christian hagiographical texts. BHO covers Arabic, Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopian hagiographical materials and remains essential for non-Latin/Greek saints. ([Internet Archive][2])

* **[E/T] BHLms / Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina Manuscripta** — Latin hagiographic manuscripts described in Bollandist catalogues; use it for manuscript-level work on saints’ lives, miracle collections, translations of relics, and legendary cycles. ([Société des Bollandistes][3])

* **[E/T] Pinakes / Πίνακες, IRHT-CNRS** — the central database for the manuscript tradition of Greek texts before the sixteenth century. It is indispensable for Greek patristics, Byzantine saints, catenae, lectionaries, monastic texts, and Greek biblical reception. ([Pinakes][4])

* **[E/T] Mirabile: Digital Archives for Medieval Culture** — Italian digital infrastructure for medieval Latin culture, including hagiographical, textual, and manuscript tools. Use it for Latin saints, sermon corpora, medieval authors, and manuscript transmission. ([Princeton LibGuides][5])

* **[E/T] e-Clavis: Christian Apocrypha** — NASSCAL’s comprehensive bibliography and clavis for Christian apocrypha; essential for infancy gospels, apocryphal acts, apostolic legends, Marian texts, apocalypses, and later legendary reception. ([NASSCAL][6])

## Eastern Christian digital tools

* **[E/T] Syriaca.org: *A Guide to Syriac Authors*** — the core reference portal for Syriac authors and texts. It is especially useful for Syriac saints, monastic writers, bishops, poets, hagiographers, and biblical interpreters. ([Syriaca][7])

* **[E/T] Qadishe: A Guide to the Syriac Saints** — digital catalogue of saints and holy persons venerated in Syriac traditions. This directly fills the “saints outside Greek/Latin” gap. ([Syriaca][8])

* **[E/T] Bibliotheca Hagiographica Syriaca Electronica / BHSE** — catalogue of more than 1,800 Syriac stories, hymns, homilies, and hagiographical texts on saints, with incipits, explicits, manuscripts, bibliography, and translations. ([Syriaca][9])

* **[E/T] HMML Reading Room / Hill Museum & Manuscript Library** — essential for Eastern Christian manuscripts. HMML’s Eastern Christian collections include digital images or microfilms of about 75,000 manuscripts from Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, including Armenian, Christian Arabic, Coptic, Ethiopic, Malayalam, Church Slavonic, and Syriac witnesses. ([HMML][10])

* **[E/T] Coptic Scriptorium** — digital corpus for Coptic texts, including Shenoute and other monastic/hagiographical material, with stable URNs and searchable linguistic annotation. ([Coptic Scriptorium Text Server][11])

* **[E/T] Clavis Aethiopica / Beta maṣāḥəft** — comprehensive repertory of Ethiopian and Eritrean literature based on manuscript evidence; vital for Ethiopic saints’ lives, miracles of Mary, monastic texts, biblical reception, and Geʿez hagiography. ([Betamasaheft][12])

* **[E/T] Index of Armenian Manuscripts** — practical portal to digitized Armenian manuscripts, useful for Armenian saints, biblical reception, liturgy, and monastic/hagiographical texts. ([Armenian Manuscripts Index][13])

# 2. Samaritanism: almost entirely missing

This is a major gap because Samaritan tradition is not merely a textual variant of the Pentateuch. It is a living Israelite-religious tradition with its own Torah, liturgy, priesthood, pilgrimage, calendar, Aramaic/Hebrew/Arabic literature, and scriptural interpretation.

## Primary and textual resources

* **[E/T] Samaritan Pentateuch manuscripts** — not optional for Pentateuch textual history or reception of Torah. Cambridge’s MS Add.1846 is a major example; Samaritan Pentateuch manuscripts preserve the Torah in Samaritan script and often require separate palaeographical and codicological expertise. ([Cambridge Digital Library][14])

* **[E/T] Samaritan manuscripts at the John Rylands Library, Manchester** — over 370 Samaritan manuscripts and letters, dating from the thirteenth to early twentieth centuries. This is a major manuscript collection for Samaritan liturgy, Torah, correspondence, and communal history. ([Digital Collections Manchester][15])

* **[E/P] *Memar Marqah*** — the central classical Samaritan theological text. Any serious account of Samaritan devotion, Moses, Torah, and Gerizim needs it.

* **[E/P] Samaritan liturgical poetry and prayer books** — crucial for lived Samaritan holiness, Passover, pilgrimage, priesthood, and the annual cycle.

* **[E/P] Samaritan Arabic commentaries and chronicles** — important for medieval and early modern Samaritan intellectual history.

## Secondary sources

* **[E] Alan D. Crown, Reinhard Pummer, and Abraham Tal, eds., *A Companion to Samaritan Studies*** — indispensable reference for Samaritan texts, history, language, religion, manuscripts, and scholarship. Mohr Siebeck has reissued it digitally. ([Mohr Siebeck][16])

* **[E] Alan D. Crown, *Samaritan Scribes and Manuscripts*** — crucial codicological and palaeographical work on Samaritan manuscript use.

* **[E] Reinhard Pummer, *The Samaritans: A Profile*** — standard modern historical and religious introduction.

* **[E] Magnar Kartveit, *The Origin of the Samaritans*** — important for origins, identity, and biblical-historical debate.

* **[E] Abraham Tal, *A Dictionary of Samaritan Aramaic*** — necessary linguistic tool.

* **[E] Moshe Florentin, *Late Samaritan Hebrew*** — required for Samaritan Hebrew linguistic study.

* **[I] Haseeb Shehadeh, Samaritan Arabic and manuscript studies** — especially important for Arabic Samaritan texts and living tradition.

* **[I] Nathan Schur, *History of the Samaritans*** — older, broad historical synthesis.

# 3. Epigraphy, papyrology, numismatics, and non-literary evidence

We discussed texts, but not enough **inscriptions, papyri, coins, seals, amulets, ostraca, curse tablets, synagogue inscriptions, funerary texts, and documentary papyri**. These often show how Scripture, divine names, saints, angels, prayers, and Jewish/Christian identities actually functioned outside elite books.

## Core tools

* **[E/T] Inscriptions of Israel/Palestine / IIP** — database collecting previously published inscriptions and English translations from Persian period through Islamic conquest. It covers Jewish, Christian, pagan, Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, and Latin inscriptions. ([Inscriptions Israel Palestine][17])

* **[E/T] Corpus Inscriptionum Iudaeae/Palaestinae / CIIP** — the major printed corpus of inscriptions from ancient Judea/Palaestina. Volume I covers Jerusalem from Alexander to the Arab conquest and includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Armenian inscriptions. ([De Gruyter Brill][18])

* **[E/T] Papyri.info / Duke Databank of Documentary Papyri** — electronic corpus of published Greek and Latin documents on papyrus, ostraca, and wooden tablets. Essential for social history, legal documents, names, religious practice, and documentary contexts. ([Papyri][19])

* **[E/T] Trismegistos** — metadata platform for ancient texts, originally papyrological but now much broader; covers multiple languages, media, and regions. Use it for provenance, dating, people, places, manuscripts, papyri, and inscriptions. ([Trismegistos][20])

## Missing references

* **[E] David Noy, *Jewish Inscriptions of Western Europe*** — indispensable for diaspora Jewish inscriptions.

* **[E] William Horbury and David Noy, *Jewish Inscriptions of Graeco-Roman Egypt*** — core for Egyptian Jewish epigraphy.

* **[E] Pieter van der Horst, *Ancient Jewish Epitaphs*** — death, identity, piety, resurrection, family, synagogue networks.

* **[I] Walter Ameling, *Inscriptiones Judaicae Orientis*** — eastern Jewish inscriptions.

* **[I] Christopher Rollston, *Writing and Literacy in the World of Ancient Israel*** — epigraphy, scribes, literacy.

* **[I] André Lemaire, Hebrew/Aramaic inscription studies** — major French epigrapher; essential for West Semitic inscriptions.

# 20. What I would add first now

The most important missing shelves to add are these:

1. **BHL / BHG / BHO / Clavis Clavium / Pinakes / HMML / Syriaca / Coptic Scriptorium / Beta maṣāḥəft** — without these, saint and reception history remains secondhand.
2. **Samaritan studies** — *Companion to Samaritan Studies*, Samaritan Pentateuch manuscripts, *Memar Marqah*, Samaritan liturgy.
3. **Epigraphy and papyrology** — CIIP, IIP, DDbDP, Trismegistos, Jewish inscriptions.
4. **Paratextual Bible** — glosses, catenae, chaptering, canon tables, concordances, marginalia.
5. **Sermons and preaching** — Old, Kienzle, Wenzel, d’Avray, Saperstein, Schneyer.
6. **Education and formation** — yeshivot, catechesis, monastic pedagogy, children’s religious formation.
7. **Liturgy and hymnody** — piyyut, chant, lectionaries, offices, synaxaria, hagiographic calendars.
8. **Non-English hagiography scholarship** — Delehaye, Aigrain, Vauchez, Boesch Gajano, Angenendt, Grundmann, Dinzelbacher, Ruh, Bremond, de Certeau.
9. **Eastern Christian saints** — Syriac Qadishe/BHSE, Coptic Scriptorium, Armenian manuscript tools, Clavis Aethiopica, Ethiopic hagiography.
10. **Jewish saintliness and tzaddik traditions** — Ben-Ami, Bilu, Pedaya, Elior, Liebes, Hellner-Eshed, Hasidic and Moroccan saint veneration.
11. **Relics, pilgrimage, tombs, sacred space** — Geary, Hahn, Bartlett, Finucane, Bitton-Ashkelony, Bilu, Rehav Rubin.
12. **Healing, demons, miracles, discernment** — Caciola, Sluhovsky, Elliott, Trachtenberg, Bohak, Harari, Chajes.
13. **Lived religion and ethnography** — Orsi, McGuire, Ammerman, Bielo, Luhrmann, Morgan, Meyer.
14. **Religious orders and institutional holiness** — Benedictine, Cistercian, Franciscan, Dominican, Carmelite, Jesuit, Hasidic courts, Mussar schools.
15. **Ordinary devotional readers** — children, women, households, lay confraternities, siddur users, Bible-study groups, Daf Yomi communities, pilgrims, shrine devotees.

The pattern in the gaps is clear: we covered **texts and interpreters** well; what still needed expansion was **how communities physically, ritually, institutionally, and bodily live with texts and holy persons**.
