Below is an **expanded per-book PhD-level reading map**. I am treating this as **additive** to the first per-book list: I include some anchors again where they define the field, but the emphasis is on **what was missing** and **what each added work contributes**.

A dissertation-level student would not necessarily read every item cover to cover, but should know **why these works matter**, where they stand in the field, and which arguments later scholarship assumes, revises, or rejects.

The backbone here is still the major technical commentary ecosystem: **ICC** for philological/textual/historical detail, **Anchor Yale** for book-by-book critical scholarship, **Hermeneia** for historical-critical and history-of-religions depth, **OTL/NTL** for critical but readable synthesis, **NIGTC** for Greek-text exegesis, **BHQ** for Hebrew textual control, **ECM/NA/UBS** for New Testament textual criticism, **SBLCS/Göttingen/NETS** for Septuagint, and **CEJL** for Early Jewish/Second Temple commentaries. ICC explicitly aims to marshal linguistic, textual, archaeological, historical, literary, and theological evidence; Anchor Yale describes itself as a long-running major critical series; Hermeneia is designed as a critical-historical commentary using philological and historical tools; OTL emphasizes historical world, ancient manuscripts, literary design, and theological exposition; NIGTC focuses on Greek-text exegesis; BHQ is the current critical manual edition of the Hebrew Bible; ECM is the major critical Greek NT project; SBLCS focuses on the Septuagint as translation; and CEJL covers Jewish texts from roughly 300 BCE–150 CE. ([Bloomsbury][1])

# Categories used below

**[C] Commentary core** — full technical commentary or commentary-like monograph.
**[TC] Textual criticism / textual history** — MT, LXX, DSS, versions, manuscript evidence.
**[COMP] Composition / redaction / sources** — literary growth, source criticism, redaction history.
**[FORM] Form, genre, tradition history** — genre, oral/traditional units, Sitz im Leben.
**[HIST] Historical / archaeological context** — realia, ancient history, social world.
**[LIT] Literary / narrative / poetics** — synchronic literary and rhetorical analysis.
**[SOC] Social, gender, ideological, postcolonial** — social location and critique.
**[THEOL] Theology / conceptual synthesis** — theological interpretation, canonical shaping, conceptual history.
**[REC] Reception history** — ancient, medieval, modern reception.
**[H] Historical classic** — still necessary even if superseded.

# I. Hebrew Bible / Old Testament

## Genesis

**A PhD student in Genesis needs control of:** source criticism, primeval history, patriarchal traditions, Joseph story, ANE myth, genealogies, land/promise, narrative poetics, textual history, and reception.

Add these:

* **[C/H] John Skinner, *Genesis* ICC** — older but still useful for classical source-critical and philological control.
* **[C] E. A. Speiser, *Genesis* AB** — adds mid-twentieth-century ANE comparative work, especially Mesopotamian and Hurrian parallels.
* **[C] Gordon J. Wenham, *Genesis 1–15; 16–50* WBC** — adds detailed evangelical-critical philology, structure, and literary coherence.
* **[C] Nahum Sarna, *Genesis* JPS** — adds Jewish philological and literary synthesis, often excellent on Hebrew style and theology.

* **[COMP] Erhard Blum, *Die Komposition der Vätergeschichte*** — essential for patriarchal narrative composition.
* **[COMP] John Van Seters, *Abraham in History and Tradition*; *Prologue to History*** — adds supplementary/compositional model and critique of patriarchal historicity.
* **[COMP] Joseph Blenkinsopp, *The Pentateuch*** — concise but important compositional synthesis.

# IX. What “PhD-level complete” adds beyond commentaries

For any single biblical book, a dissertation student should also be expected to control:

1. **Textual base:** BHQ/BHS, Göttingen/Rahlfs/NETS for LXX, DSS witnesses, Samaritan Pentateuch where relevant, Peshitta/Vulgate/Targum where relevant, ECM/NA/UBS for NT.
2. **History of interpretation:** ancient Jewish, patristic, rabbinic, medieval, Reformation, modern reception where the book has major afterlives.
3. **Ancient context:** ANE, Second Temple, Greco-Roman, or rabbinic context depending on corpus.
4. **Major methodological turns:** source/redaction criticism, form criticism, literary criticism, social-scientific work, feminist/postcolonial/ideological criticism, reception history.
5. **Field-shaping old work:** not because it is always correct, but because later scholarship is often arguing with it.
6. **Recent dissertation-adjacent literature:** book-specific articles from **JBL, VT, CBQ, JSOT, JSNT, NTS, NovT, ZAW, Biblica, HUCA, JJS, DSD, Henoch, JSJ**, and major Festschriften/edited volumes.
